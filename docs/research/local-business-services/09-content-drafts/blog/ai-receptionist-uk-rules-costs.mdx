An AI receptionist is now a product you can buy for less than a month of coffee, and the sales pages are about minutes and money. The decision underneath is about three other things: what the law says about a machine answering your phone, what it has to say in its first sentence, and whether the cheap plan is the right shape for your calls. This post takes them in that order and ends with a checklist.

## What an AI receptionist is, and is not

A voice agent that answers your line when nobody else can, holds a real conversation, does a narrow job — book, take a message, answer a short list of questions, transfer — and hands over cleanly when it cannot. It sits behind a divert, so your published number does not change, and it produces a summary of every call.

It is not the chat widget on your website, which is a different product with different rules. And it is not an outbound caller. Anything that dials customers to chase quotes or sell is a different thing in law, and the next section is why.

## Is an AI answering your phone legal in the UK?

The rules that touch automated telephony are in PECR, the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations, and they are written around calls an organisation makes. Regulation 19 forbids sending "communications comprising recorded matter for direct marketing purposes by means of an automated calling system" without the subscriber's prior consent. Regulation 21 governs "unsolicited calls for direct marketing purposes", which is where the Telephone Preference Service screen lives.

An inbound receptionist makes no calls. The caller rang you; nothing is being marketed at them; neither regulation's wording reaches the conversation. That is the clean case, and it is the case every honest product should be sold on.

The unsettled part is what happens if an AI voice ever does make a marketing call: is that a live call, or an automated one needing specific consent? The regulator has not said. In September 2025 it fined two energy firms £300,000 and £250,000 for marketing calls made with recorded, non-live speech, and treated them as automated calls. So the position is unsettled, with a lean — towards the stricter reading — and a receptionist that never dials never has to find out.

## The two sentences it has to say first

Before it does anything, a receptionist should say two things: that it is automated, and that the call is recorded.

The second is the clearer one. Tell callers up front that the call is recorded: the ICO's position is that telling them supports fairness even where consent is not strictly required, and silence is not consent. Recordings and transcripts are personal data, so your privacy notice has to say they exist and how long you keep them.

The first is not, as far as we could find, required by any UK statute specific to AI voices. It is still the right design, for a plain reason: a caller who believes they are talking to a person has been misled about who they are dealing with, and consumer law is built around not misleading people. It is also the design that ages well. If the position hardens, a receptionist that already says what it is does not need rebuilding.

Of the UK products we checked in September 2026 with published prices, none said on its page that the assistant tells callers it is an AI. That does not mean they do not; it means the page does not say, and it is the first question to ask before you buy.

Bounded to what we could read in September 2026, and with no names, because the point is the shape of the market rather than any one seller: entry plans from £10 a month plus a charge per call; unlimited-minute plans from £49 a month; a healthcare-specific product at £99 a month per four practitioners, stated ex VAT; managed plans, where someone sets it up and tunes it, from about £149 a month; and bespoke builds, including ours, priced as projects from £4,500.

Two things stood out. Across the twelve UK product pages we reached in the same September 2026 check — text-back, review and booking tools as well as receptionists — two said how VAT applies to their price, and both were receptionist products. And the cheap plans are cheap because they are software: you write the script, you set the divert, you handle the handovers, and you find out what it said on a call by reading the transcript afterwards.

## The costs the price list leaves out

**Minutes.** Unlimited plans exist; metered ones charge per minute or per call, and on the platform we use the underlying minutes are billed in US dollars. Ask what a busy month costs, in pounds, before you sign.

**The number.** A new UK number needs registering to your business, with your company details on the record. A divert from the number you already publish avoids that, and it is the option to prefer.

**The person.** Every receptionist hands calls over. Someone has to take them, and if nobody does, the product has quietly become a voicemail with better manners.

**The script.** The narrow job has to be written — services, slots, what to say when the diary is full, what not to answer. On a software plan that is your afternoon; on a managed plan it is someone else's.

**The retention decision.** Recordings pile up. Choosing how long to keep them is a decision, not a default, and it has to be made before the first call.

## When it pays

Not from a statistic on a sales page. Count your own missed calls for a week — many phone systems and mobile providers can show you — and put a rough value on the ones that were jobs or appointments. Then run the receptionist on out-of-hours only for a month and read the report: answered, booked, transferred, dropped, minutes used. If the "booked" line is worth more than the plan and the "dropped" line is near zero, widen the divert. If not, you have spent a month and learned something true about your phone, which is cheaper than guessing.

| Your situation                                                                                   | The right shape                                                       |

| Calls are quotes, appointments and opening hours                                                 | A plan                                                                |

| You need it to ring people back or chase quotes                                                  | A scoped outbound project on an evidenced consent record — not a plan |
| It has to write into a practice-management or job-management system with no standard integration | Bespoke                                                               |
| You want to hear it fail on a test number before anyone signs it off                             | Either, but insist on the test call                                   |
| Your customers are patients and their records are health data                                    | Bespoke, in an account you own                                        |

## A checklist before you sign

1. It answers inbound calls only, and the product has no outbound or callback feature you could switch on by mistake.
2. Its first sentence says it is automated and that the call is recorded — ask to hear it.
3. It hands over to a person, and you know who that person is on a Tuesday afternoon.
4. You know what a busy month costs in pounds, minutes included.
5. Your published number stays, on a divert you control.
6. It declines the questions it should decline.
7. You have chosen how long recordings are kept and your privacy notice says so.
8. The report shows counts you can check — answered, booked, transferred, dropped, minutes used.
