Ring a plumber, a garage or a salon, get no answer, and a message may arrive before you have put the phone down: sorry we missed you, reply here. Many of the businesses sending them may never have asked whether they are allowed to — and the honest answer is that it depends entirely on what the text says.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  A text back to a missed call is a service message while it tells the caller you missed them and
  how to reach you, and nothing more. Add an offer and it becomes marketing to a number you hold no
  consent for. The rule is about content, not intention, and the safe template fits in one message.
</Callout>

## "Why do I get a text message after a missed call?"

Because the business you rang has an automation watching its line. When a call rings out or hits voicemail, the system sends a text from the number you dialled, or from one tied to it, and routes whatever you reply into an inbox someone reads. Done well it is a useful thing to bolt onto a phone: the caller who would have rung the next name on the list now has a thread open with the first one.

Done badly it is a marketing message nobody consented to, sent on a schedule, to everyone who ever called. The difference is not the technology. It is a sentence.

## Service message or marketing? The ICO's test

The Information Commissioner's Office enforces the UK's electronic-marketing rules, and its guidance draws the line in plain words. Messages "for administrative or customer services purposes" — it lists reminding people how to contact you and confirming or reminding them about appointments — are service messages, not direct marketing. Then the tripwire: "if your service message has elements that are direct marketing, even if that is not the main purpose of your message, then it will count as direct marketing." Branding and a logo do not tip it over; a promotion does.

So "Sorry we missed your call — reply here or book at this link" carries nothing but service content. "Sorry we missed your call — book today and get 10% off" carries an offer, and under the ICO's test that is what makes it direct marketing. The second is the one a template drifts towards, because the person writing it is thinking about conversion rather than classification, and because the tool makes it easy.

## What PECR actually says about texts

The Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations treat a text as "electronic mail", so the email rules apply to SMS unchanged. Regulation 22 applies "to the transmission of unsolicited communications by means of electronic mail to individual subscribers" — that is, sole traders, unincorporated partnerships and anyone on a personal number — and says that, outside two exceptions, a person "shall neither transmit, nor instigate the transmission of, unsolicited communications for the purposes of direct marketing by means of electronic mail unless the recipient … has previously notified the sender that he consents".

Limited companies, LLPs and public bodies are corporate subscribers, and the consent rule does not apply to them. That distinction is an easy one to get wrong, because a mobile number tells you nothing about the legal form of the person holding it. The [CRM page](/services/crm-automation) goes through it properly; for a text-back the point is simpler — if the message is a service message, the question never arises, whoever the caller is.

Regulation 23 adds two things that every message should carry anyway: the sender's identity must not be "disguised or concealed", and there must be "a valid address to which the recipient of the communication may send a request that such communications cease". A text that names the business and says how to stop meets both.

## The soft opt-in, and why a missed call is not one

There is an exception people reach for. Regulation 22(3) allows marketing by electronic mail where the sender "has obtained the contact details of the recipient … in the course of the sale or negotiations for the sale of a product or service", the marketing is for "similar products and services only", and the recipient was given "a simple means of refusing" at collection and in every message since.

Is a missed call "negotiations for the sale"? Nobody has tested it, and a business that builds its text-back on the assumption is building on a question rather than an answer. The better design does not need the exception: keep the text a service message, and use the soft opt-in for what it was written for — customers who actually bought something, or asked to.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The one line that changes everything">
  A template that says "book today and save" has stopped being a service message. The number it went
  to was collected from a missed call, not a sale, and no consent was ever given. That is an
  unsolicited marketing text, automated, to everyone who rings out — the exact shape of the thing
  regulation 22 prohibits.
</Callout>

## The fine, and the number that matters more

Since 20 August 2025, PECR breaches carry the same ceiling as UK GDPR breaches: up to £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. At the time of writing the ICO's own guidance on electronic mail marketing carries an "under review" notice for the Data (Use and Access) Act — a useful reminder that a regulator's summary page and the law can be different things.

No six-person business is going to meet that ceiling. The number that matters is smaller and nearer: a complaint from someone who never wanted the text, a letter from the ICO asking for your lawful basis, and — the one that actually hurts — a growing list of callers who replied STOP and may not ring again. The cheapest fix is the message that never needs defending.

## A template that fits one message

> Hi, it's {Business}. Sorry we missed your call. Reply here and we'll get back to you, or book at {short link}. Reply STOP to opt out.

Five things make it work. It names who sent it. It says why. It gives the caller a way to reply and a way to book. It gives them a way to stop. And it carries no offer. With a 24-character business name and the link placeholder as printed it is 147 characters, which matters because a text is priced and delivered in 160-character segments — provided you stay within the basic character set. An emoji or a typographic dash switches the whole message to a 70-character encoding, so the same words arrive, and are billed, as two or three texts.

Of the four UK missed-call products we looked at in September 2026, two showed a sample message that carried a "book today" line or an emoji. Neither is fatal on its own; both are the small drift that ends with an offer in the template.

## Whose number is it?

The text should come from a number registered to your business, and say who it is from in its first words. The simplest way is a divert: your published number stays as it is, unanswered calls forward to a line the platform watches, and the text goes out from a number tied to your business.

If you want a new UK number instead, expect paperwork. On the platform we use, a UK long-code number needs an approved regulatory compliance bundle (its UK guidance, July 2024) — the business name, its registration and a UK business address go on the record, and a Companies House number means no further documents; a business not registered in the UK gets a UK mobile or freephone number instead. Branded sender names, the ones that show a name in place of a number, only work one way, so anything that expects a reply must come from a real number. Whatever you choose, the number should be registered to your business, not to whoever set it up.

## What to check in whatever tool you use

1. The template carries no offer, no discount and no review link — service content only.
2. It names the business and gives a way to stop.
3. Replies go to a person, and "stop" actually stops it.
4. The caller's number is not added to a campaign, a newsletter or a review request.
5. Your privacy notice says you process callers' numbers to return their calls, and for how long you keep them.
6. The number is registered to your business, and you know which line is diverted where.
7. The message fits one 160-character segment in the basic character set — no emoji, no curly quotes.
8. Nothing on the caller's side depends on the soft opt-in.

If your tool cannot do one of those, that is the thing to fix before the next missed call, not after the first complaint.
