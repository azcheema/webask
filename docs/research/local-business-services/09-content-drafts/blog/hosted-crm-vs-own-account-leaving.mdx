A hosted CRM is the easy way in: no platform to learn, no platform subscription of your own to manage, someone else configures it and you get an inbox and a calendar that work. The question that matters is the way out, and it is the question to settle before the sales conversation ends. This post answers it in the vendor's own words, dated, for the platform we use, because the conditions are specific and they change.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  In a hosted sub-account you stay the controller of your customers' data and the agency processes
  it for you under a written contract, on US-hosted infrastructure. If you leave, the sub-account
  can be transferred to an agency account of your own: contacts, conversations, sites, funnels,
  calendars, users, workflows and API keys move, and automations should be expected to arrive as
  drafts (the eject article says so; the transfer guide is silent); connected accounts, email
  sending, Stripe, white-label domains and smart lists do not move; add-on subscriptions are
  cancelled first; numbers move only where both sides use the platform's telephony. Write the exit
  checklist on day one.
</Callout>

## Two ways to run a CRM

In your own account: you hold the subscription, the agency configures it as a project and leaves, and nothing about your access depends on them. In a hosted sub-account: the agency holds an agency-level licence, your business is one sub-account under it, and the agency runs it month to month. The first is the default we recommend and the [CRM page](/services/crm-automation) is where it is built; the second is the labelled exception behind our monthly plans. Both are legitimate. Only one of them needs an exit clause.

## What "hosted" means in data-protection terms

You are the controller of your customers' data; the agency processes it for you, and UK GDPR Article 28 requires that to happen under a written contract. The platform's own data processing agreement names the processor as HighLevel, Inc., a Texas corporation, and rests the transfer on the company's certification under the UK Extension to the EU-US Data Privacy Framework, with the UK Addendum to the standard contractual clauses as the fallback; its security overview states that the infrastructure sits in the United States; sub-processor changes come with thirty days' notice and a right to object, and customer data is deleted or returned on termination. As at 23 September 2026 the company's Data Privacy Framework listing read "Active – Re-certification under Review", which is worth re-checking on the day you sign.

None of that is a reason not to host. It is the set of facts a hosted-plan agreement should state rather than leave you to discover, and it is why "your data stays in the UK" is not a sentence an agency on this platform can say.

## What moves when a sub-account is transferred

The platform's transfer guide, modified 7 August 2026, lists what moves between agencies: contacts, conversations, websites, funnels, calendars, users, workflows and API keys. "Only the Agency Owner can request or complete a sub-account transfer" — the guide adds that an Agency Admin can be granted the permission — and the receiving agency must be "active and eligible to accept the additional sub-account" with "required account capacity, compliance add-ons, and active account status". "Phone numbers transfer with the sub-account" where both agencies use the platform's telephony, and "Eligible A2P registration stays with the numbers".

## What arrives as drafts, and what does not move

The eject article — the route by which a client becomes their own agency — is dated 22 April 2026 and is blunter. "All automation will be transferred over. Note: They will all be set to Draft." So every workflow arrives switched off, to be checked and switched on by you or your new agency. "All auth connections will be deleted" — Google, Facebook, Instagram and the rest are reconnected on your side. "Smartlists do not get transferred." Stripe fields are cleared, email sending through the agency's Mailgun or SMTP does not come with you, and "If your agency has a whitelabel domain … that also does not transfer automatically."

The transfer guide lists workflows and says nothing about their state. The exit checklist plans for the drafts case, because a business that expects automations to arrive running and finds them paused has been let down twice.

## Phone numbers

Where both agencies use the platform's own telephony, numbers move with the sub-account and keep their registration status. Where they do not, the eject article says "manual coordination with Twilio" is required — which is a project, not a setting. It is one more reason the plans run on a divert from the number you already publish: the number that matters was never in the account to begin with.

## Add-ons cancelled first

"All active subscriptions like Wordpress, Yext, Whatsapp, Dedicated IP etc. will have to be cancelled" before an ejection, and "Any SaaS sub-account will need to have SaaS disabled". In plain terms: your WhatsApp channel, your listings sync and any hosted WordPress site are cancelled before the move and re-subscribed on the other side, so anything that lives inside them is exported first.

## What the eject route costs

The eject article names no transfer fee. It says the nominated person is invited to sign up for the "$97/month plan" and becomes the new agency owner — so leaving to run your own account means holding an agency-level subscription of your own, billed in dollars, or finding another agency to receive the sub-account. Both are real options; neither is free.

## A plain export

Before or instead of any of that, an export: contacts with their conversation history and the forms they submitted, in files you can open without the platform. It does not carry the automations or the pages, and it does not need to. It is the guarantee that whatever happens to the transfer, the relationships come with you.

## The exit checklist to write on day one

1. Who is the controller, who is the processor, which contract says so, and where the data is hosted.
2. What moves in a transfer, what arrives as drafts, and what is reconnected on your side.
3. Which number is on a divert, which is in the account, and whether both sides use the platform's telephony.
4. Which add-ons are live and must be cancelled and re-subscribed.
5. What a plain export contains and how quickly you get it.
6. What the exit costs: a receiving agency, or your own agency subscription.
7. The date the checklist was written and the vendor articles it was written from.
