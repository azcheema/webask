Somewhere between the booking and the chair, a customer forgets. A text the day before fixes a good deal of that, and it is an automated message many UK small businesses already send without ever asking whether they are allowed to. The answer is yes, on a condition that is easy to state and easy to break: the reminder has to stay a reminder.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  A reminder that says when the appointment is and how to change it is a service message, and the
  ICO lists it as one. Add an offer, a review link or a "reply YES to hear about deals" and it
  becomes direct marketing to a number you may hold no consent for. Name the sender, give a way to
  stop, keep it to one text, and send it because of the appointment, never because of a campaign.
</Callout>

## "Are appointment reminders marketing?"

Not while they remind. The Information Commissioner's Office, which enforces the UK's electronic-marketing rules, describes messages sent "for administrative or customer services purposes" as service messages, and its own examples are the ones a small business sends every day: to "confirm or remind them about appointments", to "remind people how to contact you in case of a problem", to "check their contact details are correct", to "update them on your terms or conditions". None of those is direct marketing, and none needs marketing consent.

Then the line that decides everything: "if your service message has elements that are direct marketing, even if that is not the main purpose of your message, then it will count as direct marketing." The classification is about content, not intention, and not about what the message is called in your software.

## The one line that turns a reminder into an advert

"See you Tuesday at 10." A service message. "See you Tuesday at 10 — and book your winter service this month for £20 off." An advert, sent to a number collected for an appointment, with no consent for marketing behind it. The second version is where a reminder ends up when the empty slot next month is on the writer's mind.

There is a quieter version of the same mistake. The ICO also lists "contacting people to ask them for consent to direct marketing" among direct marketing purposes, with the example of a hotel emailing former guests to ask whether they would like offers: the email contains no offer, and it is still marketing. So "reply YES to hear about our deals" on the end of a reminder is not a neutral question. It is the message you were trying not to send.

## What every reminder should carry anyway

PECR requires two things of a marketing message — that the sender's identity is not "disguised or concealed", and "a valid address" to which the recipient can ask for the messages to stop. A service message is not bound by that rule, and a good one meets it anyway: the business named, the appointment stated, a way to change or cancel it, and a way to stop the reminders. If someone replies STOP to a reminder, the reminders stop; they have chosen to remember for themselves, and a business that keeps texting has turned a courtesy into a nuisance.

## Who may receive one

Anyone with an appointment. A lawful basis sits in the appointment itself: UK GDPR Article 6(1)(b) covers processing that "is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party", and a reminder about a booking the customer made is part of delivering it. Your privacy notice needs to say that you send them, and for how long you keep the number. What you do not need is marketing consent, for as long as the reminder stays a reminder — and the same number may still be one you cannot lawfully market to, which is the whole reason the two kinds of message must never share a template.

## Three templates

**By text.** One segment, in the basic character set — no emoji, no typographic dashes, which force the 70-character encoding and bill the message as two or three parts:

> Hi Sam, a reminder of your appointment with {Practice} on Tue 1 Dec at 10:30. Reply C to confirm or call 0161 496 0123 to change it. Reply STOP to opt out.

That example is 155 characters with a ten-character placeholder for the practice name. Sender, appointment, a way to confirm, a way to change, a way to stop, and no offer.

**By email.** Subject line: "Your appointment on Tuesday 1 December". Body: the same five things, the address or the video link, what to bring, and how to cancel, with nothing about anything else. An email reminder with a promotional banner is a marketing email in a reminder's clothing.

**By WhatsApp.** A reminder is sent as a template message, and Meta prices template messages per delivery by category; a reminder sits in the category Meta uses for transaction and appointment updates rather than the marketing one, which is cheaper — content decides the price on WhatsApp the way it decides the classification under PECR. Two things to know. WhatsApp needs the customer's opt-in to WhatsApp messages from you before anything is sent there, whatever the category. And from 1 October 2026 Meta charges for the free-form "service" replies that follow, which "have not been charged since November 2024", so the conversation a reminder opens is no longer free either.

## Dental practices: recall and marketing are two flows

A recall reminder is a clinical courtesy that every patient should receive; a promotional message is an advert that only patients who may receive it should get. If both travel down the same pipeline, one day the recall carries the promotion and the practice has sent marketing to its whole list. The flows are built separately, with their own consent state, and the reminder never carries the offer. The [dental practices](/industries/dental-practices) page sets out the rest of that position.

## Garages: the government offers a free one

Any driver can sign up on GOV.UK for "free MOT reminders by text message or email", sent "one month before your car, van or motorcycle MOT is due". A garage's reminder therefore competes with a free government one, and it earns its place with the two things the government's cannot carry: a booking link and your price. Tied to the vehicle's due date, sent from your own number to your own customers, with a way to stop — and with no offer, for the reason above.

## No-shows: what to count

Not a percentage from a vendor page. Reminders sent, confirmations received, appointments changed, and no-shows, week by week, from the calendar that runs it. If the no-show line does not move after a month of reminders, the timing is wrong for that service — a day before for one kind of appointment, an hour before for another — and the counts are how you find out. A figure that arrives before the reminders do is not yours.

## A checklist for any booking tool

1. The reminder says who, when, where and how to change it, and nothing promotional.
2. No offer, no review link, and no "reply YES to hear about offers".
3. It names the business and gives a way to stop; STOP is honoured.
4. It goes only to people with an appointment, triggered by the appointment.
5. Your privacy notice says you send reminders and how long you keep the number.
6. Reminders and marketing live in separate flows with separate consent.
7. The text fits one 160-character segment in the basic character set.
8. On WhatsApp, the customer opted in to WhatsApp messages first.
