The question arrives as "do I need a website or a landing page", and it is the wrong question, because it asks about the thing rather than the traffic. Ask instead where the visitors are coming from. Traffic you send — from an advert, an email, a QR code on the van — needs a page to land on. Traffic you have to earn — from search, from someone typing your name — needs a website to find. Answer that, and the rest follows.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  A landing page has one job and no navigation, for visitors you sent. A website serves everyone who
  arrives from anywhere, carries the details the law expects, and is what search engines rank. If
  you are running one campaign and have no site, build the page; if people search for what you do,
  build the site; if both, the page first while the site is built. Price a page as a page.
</Callout>

## The question behind the question

Sent traffic knows why it is there. Someone who clicked an advert for a boiler service in January wants one thing, and a page that offers that thing and nothing else serves them best. Earned traffic does not: a person searching "plumber near me" might want an emergency, a quote, a review, or your opening hours, and they arrive at whichever page the search engine chose. A landing page cannot serve that person, and a website cannot serve the first one as well as a page can.

## What a landing page is for

One job. A campaign to land, an offer to take up, a booking to make, a guide to download. No navigation, because every link away is a visitor lost; one form or one calendar, wired into the CRM with consent captured at entry; a thank-you message that says what happens next. It is measured on one number, the thing it asked for, and it is switched off when the campaign ends.

## What a website is for

Being found, and being trusted. It serves every kind of visitor, so it has navigation, service pages, an about page, a way to contact you and the details the law expects a trading site to carry: a limited company's registered name, number, place of registration and registered office; anyone's trading name, geographic address and email; a VAT number where registered; and prices that say whether they include VAT. It is what search engines rank, what a customer checks before they ring, and the only asset in the pair that is still working in three years.

## "Landing page design cost" — what moves it

Three things move the price of a page — builder or code, copy edited or written, one page or a short funnel. Your industry does not, and we make no promise about conversion, because that depends on the traffic you send and the offer you make.

For scale, the figures we already publish for the other half of the decision: a custom website from us starts at £3,500; a competent UK freelancer builds a good four- or five-page site for £1,200–£2,000; a subscription builder costs £240–£360 a year and answers a cheaper question first — does anyone want this? A landing page is priced as a page, on the [pricing page](/pricing), and it sits below all of those.

## Builder or code: the trade stated honestly

The builder-or-code trade in full is on the landing pages page. The short version for this decision: a builder page is quick to make and duplicable, carries the builder's own scripts, and is not measured against the speed floor our coded builds are; a coded page takes longer to build and costs more, and it is the one we measure against that floor. Choose by where the traffic comes from — sent traffic tolerates the builder, earned traffic wants code — and remember that either page is measured after launch, never promised before.

## The rules both must meet

**Cookies.** Both must ask before setting anything non-essential, with accept and reject given equal prominence, and both can use the exceptions in force since 5 February 2026 — statistics to improve the page, with plain information and a simple way to object, and remembered preferences — while an advertising pixel still waits for a yes. The landing pages page has the detail; the rule is the same size on one page as on forty.

**Prices.** Any price on a page must be indicated "clearly and unambiguously", including "whether they are inclusive of tax". An offer page that shows a figure and only reveals VAT at the form is what that rule exists to prevent.

**Disclosures.** A landing page for a limited company still carries the company's details, and a page that takes a deposit or a booking at a distance carries the cancellation information the customer is entitled to. A single page is not a smaller obligation.

## A decision table

| Your situation                                                            | Build first                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| You are running one campaign and have no site                             | The page, in the builder, first                                                              |
| People search for what you do and you have no site                        | The site; a page only if a campaign cannot wait                                              |
| You have a good site and a campaign it cannot host quickly                | The page, in code alongside the site, or in the builder if speed matters more than the floor |
| You have a poor site and a campaign                                       | The page now, the site next; the page keeps working while the site is rebuilt                |
| The offer needs a services page, an about page and reviews to be believed | The site, with the offer as a section of it                                                  |
| Work arrives by referral and the site only needs a phone number and hours | Neither from us; the cheapest credible option is the correct one                             |

## If you need both: the order of operations

Page first, because it is the smaller build and it earns while the site is built. Then the site, with the page's form and calendar moved into it rather than rebuilt, and the campaign's tags kept so the history is not lost. The page is retired when the site can carry the offer, or kept as the campaign's landing spot with the site's navigation deliberately left off.

## A checklist before you commission either

1. Where does the traffic come from — sent, or earned?
2. Is it one job, or many?
3. Builder or code, and is the speed trade acceptable for this traffic?
4. Who owns the page, the form data, the tags and the domain? (You do, or walk away.)
5. Does the page carry the cookie consent split, the VAT position and the company details?
6. What is the one number the page is measured on?
7. What happens to the page when the campaign ends?
