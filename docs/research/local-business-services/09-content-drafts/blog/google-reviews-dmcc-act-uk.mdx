Reviews used to be a marketing question. Since 6 April 2025 they have been a consumer-law question with a regulator attached, and the tool you use to send review requests may well predate it. This post is the rule, quoted, and then a checklist you can hold any tool up against — including one you set up yourself.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  Ask every customer, offer nothing for it, and show what comes back as it is. The CMA's guidance
  says asking customers generally is fine; asking only the happy ones, pausing requests when a bad
  run comes in, or hiding low scores is not. Google's policy tells merchants not to take the same
  shortcuts. Dentists may not offer an incentive at all.
</Callout>

## What changed on 6 April 2025

The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 made three things unlawful in the UK: writing or commissioning fake consumer reviews, concealing that a review was incentivised, and publishing reviews in a misleading way. The Competition and Markets Authority can now determine a breach itself and fine directly — up to 10% of global annual turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher, for a business, and up to £150,000 for an individual — without going to court. In March 2026 it opened investigations into five named businesses over how they handled reviews.

The Act is about consumer reviews wherever they appear, so it covers the ones on your Google profile, on a review platform, and on your own site.

## The four things the CMA's guidance names

The CMA published its guidance, CMA208, on 4 April 2025. Four passages decide how a request programme has to be built.

**Cherry-picking who gets asked.** "Cherry picking positive reviews for publication over negative ones might be done either through suppressing negative reviews that have been submitted or by encouraging just those who are satisfied to leave reviews." Asking only the customers you expect to be happy is named as a way of doing the thing the Act prohibits.

**Stopping and starting.** Traders should not interfere with "the ability and willingness of reviewers to leave negative reviews in the first place", including "by arbitrarily stopping and starting review invitations". Pausing the requests after a bad week is on the list.

**Presenting reviews misleadingly.** "A trader may infringe the law if they suppress genuine negative or positive reviews, selectively promote positive or negative reviews or omit information around how reviews have been written." A wall of five-star reviews with the others left off is the textbook case.

**Incentives.** They are not banned. They must be disclosed: a business that offers something for a review "must: (a) tell consumers that the review has been incentivised, and (b) the review must still reflect the reviewer's genuine experience", and the label has to be "prominently as incentivised, namely, as an advert". A discount for a review that nobody can see was incentivised is the banned practice.

## What is still allowed — the line that matters

The same guidance draws the safe line in one sentence: asking "without predetermining the contents or sentiment expressed in the review, for example by merely emailing customers generally to ask if they wish to provide a review, is not prohibited under the banned practice". Every customer, the same request, no screening. That is the whole permitted method, and it happens to be the one that produces a rating you can defend.

## Google's rules sit on top

A Google review also lives inside Google's policy for user-contributed content, and it forbids the same shortcuts from the platform's side. Google says merchants "should not discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers", "should not offer incentives – such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services - in exchange for posting any review or revision or removal of a negative review", and "should not require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises". A programme that breaks Google's policy risks the reviews and the profile; one that breaks the Act risks the fine. Every shortcut above breaks at least one of them; screening who is asked, and an incentive nobody can see, break both.

## "Rate us first" — the feature to switch off

A review tool may offer to ask a customer to score you privately and then send the public review link only to the customers who scored you well. It is the CMA's cherry-picking passage and Google's "selectively solicit" sentence implemented as software. If your tool has it, switch it off. If your tool cannot run without it, change tools.

Of the six review platforms we checked on 24 September 2026, one pricing page mentioned the Act, as a labelling feature; none mentioned incentives or review gating. The rule is not in the sales copy, which is a reason to read this post rather than the feature list.

## Is a review request a marketing message?

The ICO, which enforces the UK's electronic-marketing rules, has not said. Its guidance does say that a message with promotional elements counts as direct marketing "even if that is not the main purpose", and that a text is "electronic mail" under the same rules as email. So the safe design is a request that asks for a review and nothing else — no offer, no "while you're there", no link to this month's promotion — sent to people you have just done business with. Add a promotion and you have sent a marketing message, and the consent rules for individuals apply to it.

## Dentists have a stricter rule

For a dental practice there is a second layer: the General Dental Council's standards call for genuine reviews with no incentives at all. Not disclosed incentives — none. A request programme for a practice therefore offers nothing, ever, and that is the simplest way to comply with both the regulator and the Act.

## If you show reviews on your site

Embedding reviews on your own site makes you a publisher of them, and the guidance treats a business showing another platform's reviews as a "second-party publisher" that "should review the first-party publisher's policies and arrangements … to satisfy themselves, on reasonable grounds, that those arrangements meet the duty". In practice: show the rating the platform calculates and the reviews it has published, in the order it publishes them, and do not filter. A widget set to four stars and above is the selective promotion the guidance describes, built by you.

## Who is on the hook

The tool is not a shield. The CMA's short guide says you "can make agreements with a third party to monitor reviews and information, but you are still responsible for taking your own steps to prevent and remove banned content", and the full guidance names "marketing companies, individuals acting on behalf of traders" among those who can commit the banned practice by commissioning. If an agency runs your review requests, the rules run through the agency and through you. ## A checklist for any review tool

1. Every customer gets the request — no screening, no "rate us first", no private score before the public link.
2. Requests run continuously — no pausing after a bad review.
3. Nothing is offered for a review. If your sector allows an incentive and you insist on one, the review carries a prominent label that it was incentivised.
4. The request asks for a review and nothing else — no promotion in the message.
5. Nobody is asked to review on the premises while staff are watching.
6. Replies are written by a person and posted only after approval; no reply argues with the customer in public.
7. Any widget on your site shows the platform's rating and reviews as published, unfiltered.
8. If a third party runs the requests, you have read how, and you know that you remain responsible.
