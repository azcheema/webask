"Database reactivation" is a phrase from the platform world. It means taking every contact a business has ever collected and sending them something to bring them back, and in the software that sells it, it is a template you can switch on in an afternoon. What the template cannot do is tell you who on the list you are allowed to message. That is the whole job, and it comes first.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  Sort the list into three piles before anything is sent: people who consented, people who bought or
  enquired and were given a way to refuse, and everyone else. Message the first two. Do not email or
  text the third pile to ask permission, because the ICO treats that request as marketing. Reach
  them by post, by a live call after a TPS check, or the next time they walk in — or leave them.
</Callout>

## Where the phrase comes from, and what it is used to mean

On the platforms that sell it, a reactivation campaign is an automation: a message with an offer to every contact who has not bought recently, sometimes followed by two or three more. Its appeal is that it is cheap and the list is already there. In the UK it is also the single easiest way to send several thousand unsolicited marketing messages in one click, because the list was never sorted.

## The three piles

The Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations treat a text as electronic mail, so the same rule covers email and SMS. Regulation 22 applies to "individual subscribers" — sole traders, ordinary partnerships and anyone on a personal address or number — and says a person "shall neither transmit, nor instigate the transmission of, unsolicited communications for the purposes of direct marketing by means of electronic mail unless the recipient of the electronic mail has previously notified the sender that he consents". So:

**Pile one: consent on record.** They ticked an unticked box, or said yes in a way you can show, for this kind of message from you. Message them, with a way to stop in every message.

**Pile two: the soft opt-in.** The regulation's exception, in its own words: you obtained their details "in the course of the sale or negotiations for the sale of a product or service", the marketing is "in respect of that person's similar products and services only", and they were given "a simple means of refusing" when the details were collected and in every message since. The ICO adds the useful gloss that "A person doesn't need to actually buy anything from you. It's enough if 'negotiations for the sale' took place", with the examples of "signing up to a free trial of your product or service, requesting a quote or asking for more details about what you offer". Message them, about similar things, with the opt-out in every message. If nobody offered a way to refuse when the details were taken, the exception was never available, and the record belongs in pile three.

**Pile three: nothing.** A number from a missed call (whether a missed call counts as "negotiations for the sale" is untested, so treat it as though it does not), an address from a networking event, a contact somebody typed in from a business card, anything from a list you bought — the ICO is explicit that "you must not use the soft opt-ins to send unsolicited electronic mail marketing to people on a bought-in marketing list". Nothing on this pile may be marketed to by email or text.

## Corporate subscribers: the pile that needs neither

The consent rule protects individuals. For a limited company, an LLP or a public body, the ICO says plainly: "You can send unsolicited electronic mail marketing to corporate subscribers without consent or a soft opt-in." The trap is that the legal form of the recipient decides it, not the shape of the address. A sole trader on a company-looking domain is an individual; a limited company on a personal-looking one is corporate. Sort by what the business is, and where you do not know, treat the record as an individual.

## Why you cannot email or text people to ask permission

The obvious fix for pile three is to send everyone a friendly note asking whether they would like to hear from you. The ICO closes that door in one line: among direct marketing purposes it lists "contacting people to ask them for consent to direct marketing", and its example is a hotel emailing former guests to ask whether they would like to receive offers — the email contains no offer, and "the hotel is still sending it for direct marketing purposes". A request for consent, sent by the channel you have no consent for, is the message you may not send.

## What you can do with pile three

Four things, all lawful, none automated. A letter, because the electronic-mail rule does not cover post and a well-written letter to a lapsed customer still works. A live phone call, which regulation 21 allows to numbers not registered with the Telephone Preference Service, screened against the register that the TPS says should be checked every 28 days. The next time they walk in, when a person can ask another person. Or nothing, which is the right answer for a contact who last heard from you six years ago and would not remember why.

## What a reactivation message says

A reason to come back, in plain words: the service they used, what has changed, how to book. The sender named. A way to stop, because regulation 23 requires both and because a message without them is the kind that gets reported. And for two kinds of business, a line drawn by someone else's rules. A clinic's message promotes the consultation and never names, or hints at, a prescription-only medicine, because the advertising code treats an automated message as an advert. A solicitor's firm messages current and former clients only, because its code of conduct forbids unsolicited approaches to the public.

## What to count

Not the size of the list. Count the records that survived the sort — that is the mailable list, and it is smaller than the contact count on the dashboard — then replies, bookings and opt-outs from the campaign. A reactivation that produced a handful of bookings from a few hundred lawful records is a result you can repeat; one that produced more from a few thousand unsorted ones is a complaint you have not received yet.

## A checklist before any reactivation campaign

1. Every record is tagged by legal form: corporate, or individual.
2. Every individual record carries its basis — consent, or the soft opt-in with the refusal offered at collection — or it is parked.
3. Nothing from a bought, rented or scraped list is on the send.
4. Pile three is not emailed or texted, not even to ask.
5. The message names the sender, gives a reason to come back and a way to stop.
6. Clinic messages promote the consultation; law-firm messages go to existing clients only.
7. The report counts the mailable list, replies, bookings and opt-outs.
