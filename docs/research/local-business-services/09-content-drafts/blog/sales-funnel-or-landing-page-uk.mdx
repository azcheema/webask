"How much does a sales funnel cost?" was one of the questions Google showed under a UK search for funnel design on 24 September 2026, and it has no answer until "funnel" means something. Agencies use the word for a five-stage marketing model, software vendors use it for a page builder, and both are on the same results page. For a local business the useful meaning is narrower and cheaper than either: a funnel is a landing page with two more pages behind it. Once it is that, the cost question becomes a question about pages, and the build-first question answers itself.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  A landing page is one page with one job. A three-step funnel is that page, a booking or offer page
  behind it, and a thank-you page that confirms and measures. Build the one page first unless you
  already have traffic and an offer that needs a second step. What moves the cost is the number of
  pages, builder-or-code and who writes the copy, not the word "funnel".
</Callout>

## What people mean by a sales funnel

The five-stage version, from awareness to purchase and on to loyalty, is a way of describing how customers move, and it is a fair description. It is not a thing you buy. What a builder or an agency delivers when you commission "a funnel" is a short sequence of pages with an automation behind them, and the sequence is the part that costs money. The stages are free.

The question "is a sales funnel still relevant today?" sat under the same search, and the honest answer is that the word has dated while the sequence has not. A customer who arrives from an advert still needs a page that matches the advert, a way to act, and a confirmation. Whether you call that a funnel is up to you.

## The three-step funnel, page by page

**The landing page.** One offer, one action, one form or calendar. It matches whatever sent the visitor, whether an advert, a post or a text, and it does not have a menu inviting them elsewhere.

**The offer or booking page.** The step the landing page leads to: the calendar with real availability, or the price and the "book" button, or the one-question form that qualifies the enquiry. It exists because some offers need a second decision before the booking, and putting both decisions on one page makes neither clear.

**The thank-you page.** It confirms what just happened, says what happens next, and fires the event that lets you count it. It is the cheapest page of the three and the one that makes the other two measurable.

Behind the pages sits the automation: the confirmation message, the reminder, the follow-up to someone who started and stopped. That is CRM work, and it is the same work whether there are three pages or one.

## When one page is enough

If the action is a booking or a call, and the offer is clear enough to book without a second explanation, the landing page with the calendar on it is the funnel. The thank-you step becomes the confirmation screen the calendar already shows. The reason to add a page is that the offer needs explaining or qualifying between the click and the booking; where it does not, the extra pages are extra places to leave.

Build the one page first. If the report at thirty days shows people arriving and not booking, you have learned what the second page is for, and you have a page to attach it to.

## What moves the cost

Four things, in roughly this order.

1. **Pages.** One page, or three. Each page is designed, built, wired and checked, so three is not three times one but it is not one either.
2. **Builder or code.** A page built in the builder inside your CRM account is quicker and lives where your automations live; a page built in code alongside a site we built is measured against the same speed floor as the site, built to the same accessibility standard, and costs more for it. Which is right depends on where the page will live and how long it will run.
3. **Copy.** Editing copy you supply is inside the price; writing it from a brief is an addition, and it is the addition that decides whether the page says one thing clearly.
4. **Wiring and measurement.** The form or calendar into the CRM, consent captured at entry, payment if there is one, the events and tags that make the thank-you page count, and the report at thirty days.

## The rules all three pages must meet

A funnel is three pages of advertising, and the rules that apply to a website apply to each of them.

- **Cookies.** Since 5 February 2026 the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 allows cookies for statistical purposes and for remembering preferences without consent, provided there is a simple way to opt out; anything used for advertising or profiling still needs consent before it is set. A funnel that fires an advertising pixel on the landing page is exactly where that rule bites.
- **Prices.** If a price appears, the E-Commerce Regulations require it to be indicated "clearly and unambiguously", including "whether they are inclusive of tax". That is why our own figures carry "+ VAT where applicable", and why an offer page that says "£99" and nothing else is a page with a problem.
- **Distance sales.** If the offer is bought on the page rather than booked, the Consumer Contracts Regulations' information and cancellation duties apply: the customer is told about the 14-day cancellation right before they are bound, and given it in writing afterwards.
- **Who you are.** Every page that trades carries the business's name, address and, where registered, its company number and VAT number. A landing page with no footer is a landing page that forgot this.

## Builder or code

The builder page lives in the account or plan that runs your CRM, so the form, the calendar and the automation are already connected, and the page can be handed over intact if you leave. The coded page lives beside a site we built and is measured against the same speed floor, and built to the same accessibility standard, as the site; it is the right tool when the page will run for years and the traffic is earned rather than bought. Neither is "better"; the question is where the page should live.

## A decision table

| You have…                                          | Build                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| An offer that can be booked straight from the page | One landing page with the calendar on it                       |
| An offer that needs explaining before the booking  | A landing page and an offer page, then the thank-you           |
| Something sold on the page, with payment           | The three-step funnel, with the distance-sale wording built in |
| No traffic yet and no advert planned               | Neither; the website and the profile come first                |
| A page that already gets visits and no bookings    | The second page, attached to the one you have                  |

## A checklist before you commission a funnel

- Can the visitor act on the landing page alone? If yes, start there.
- What sends the traffic, and does the page match it word for word?
- Who writes the copy, and is that inside the price?
- Where will the page live in two years, and who owns it?
- Which cookies fire on the first page, and does each one have a lawful footing?
- Does every price say what it includes?
- What event fires on the thank-you page, and who reads the report?

## Where to start

None of this is legal advice; it is the rules a page must meet, stated plainly, and a build order. If you have an offer and a source of traffic, one page with the calendar on it is the first build, and the funnel is what you add when the report tells you to.
