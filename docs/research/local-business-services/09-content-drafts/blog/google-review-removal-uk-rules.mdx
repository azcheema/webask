Somebody has left a review that is wrong, unfair or plainly not from a customer, and the first search is how to contact Google to take it down. The short answer is that there is no phone line for it and no fee that buys it. There is a report button, a policy that decides what the report can achieve, and a tool that shows you where the report has got to. This post walks through those three things, and then says what to do about the review that stays.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  Google removes a review only when it breaks Google's own content policy: fake or misleading
  content, fake engagement, off-topic content, harassment and the other listed categories. A review
  that is negative, unfair or one-sided but genuine stays up. Report the ones that break policy,
  reply to the ones that do not, and treat anyone selling guaranteed removal as selling something
  Google does not offer.
</Callout>

## "How do I contact Google to take down a review?"

You do not, in the sense the question means. There is no support number for reviews and no email address that reaches a person who can delete one. The route is the report button on the review itself: in your Business Profile, "next to the review you'd like to flag, select Report", then choose the reason from Google's list. The report goes to Google's moderation, not to a sales team, and the same page is blunt about what it is for: "Do not report a review just because you disagree with it or dislike it."

Once you have reported a review, the Reviews Management Tool in the profile shows its status. Google's own labels are "Decision pending", "Report reviewed - no policy violation" and "Escalated - check your email for updates". If the answer is that no policy was broken, the tool allows "a one-time appeal". That is the whole official machinery: one report, one status, one appeal.

## What Google will remove, and what it will not

The page that describes reporting says it in one sentence: "Only those that violate Google policies are eligible for removal." The policies are the Maps user-generated content rules, and their headings are the kinds of content Google takes down: fake and misleading content and reviews, fake engagement, rating manipulation, impersonation, misinformation, harassment, hate speech, offensive content, personal information, off-topic content, advertising and solicitation, and the rest of the list. A report succeeds when the review fits one of those headings and Google agrees that it does.

What is not on the list matters more. "Negative" is not a category. "Unfair" is not a category. "Exaggerated" is not a category. A one-star review from a customer who was genuinely served, and who has described their experience harshly, is not something the policy removes, and a report against it will come back as "no policy violation".

The review from someone who was never a customer is the hard case, and it turns on which reason you pick. The policy has headings for fake and misleading content and for fake engagement, and a review from a competitor or a former employee, or from an account that posts the same review across many businesses, is reported under those. A grudge is not a category on its own; the review has to fit one that is. You cannot prove a negative in the report form, and Google does not phone you for evidence, so the report you write is the case you make. Pick the category the review actually fits, and pick it once.

## The review that says something untrue

A review that makes a false factual claim about you may be defamatory, and defamation is law rather than policy. Defamation is a solicitor's matter, and sometimes a court's, and it sits outside the review-report tool and outside this post. Nothing on this page is legal advice, and the honest thing to say is that the legal route is slow and costly, and the report button and the reply come first.

## What the reviews tool shows while you wait

Google's page says that "review evaluation typically takes several days". That is the only timescale Google gives, so it is the only one we repeat. While the status reads "Decision pending", there is nothing to do; Google's page describes one report, a status that changes and a one-time appeal, and nothing in it suggests a second report moves the first, and reporting a batch of reviews you dislike in the hope one sticks is the pattern the "do not report a review just because you disagree with it" sentence exists to stop.

Keep a note of the date you reported each review, the reason you chose and the status when it changed. It is a small discipline that stops the same review being reported twice by two people and gives you something to refer to if you use the one appeal.

## Why nobody can sell you removal

Only Google removes reviews from Google. A service that offers removal can only file the report you can file yourself; nobody outside Google decides the outcome. Of the UK review-management pages we read on 24 September 2026, one sold removal with a money-back promise. We do not sell that, because nobody outside Google decides what Google removes, and a promise about someone else's decision is not a promise we can keep.

What a review programme can do is narrower and more useful: report the reviews that break policy, with the right reason and a record; draft a reply to the ones that do not; and keep genuine reviews coming from every customer, so that one bad review sits among many real ones rather than on its own. That last part is the one within your control, and it does not need anything removed.

## The reviews you publish yourself are a different law

Everything above is about Google's site. If you also show reviews on your own website, through a widget or a testimonials page, a second set of rules applies, and they are yours to follow rather than Google's. Since 6 April 2025 the Act, and the CMA's guidance on it (CMA208, 4 April 2025), treat a business that publishes consumer reviews as responsible for what it shows: suppressing genuine negative reviews or selectively promoting the positive ones is one of the practices the guidance names. A widget that shows your published rating and reviews as they are is fine. A widget that shows the five-star ones and hides the rest is not, whatever Google does with the originals. Aesthetic and beauty clinics have a further question about review widgets that name treatments; on the sites we build, their reviews are shown on Google, not embedded, until the advertising position on treatment names is settled.

## What helps instead: the reply

Google's own guidance for local ranking says that "when you reply to customer reviews, it shows that you value their feedback". The reply to a bad review is read by the next customer, not by the reviewer, and it is written for them. Short, factual, unheated. It acknowledges the experience, says what you would do differently or invites the person to contact you directly, and stops. It never argues the details, and it never confirms anything about the reviewer that a stranger could not already see, so treat it as a rule: confirming that someone was a customer, a patient or a tenant discloses something the review itself did not, and that is their personal data.

A steady flow of genuine reviews does the rest. Ask everyone, incentivise nobody, hide nothing: that is the rule on the asking side, and it is the whole reason a single unfair review stops mattering.

## A checklist before you press "report"

- Is it a policy violation, or is it a negative review? Only the first is eligible.
- Which heading does it fit: fake or misleading content, fake engagement, off-topic, harassment? Choose one.
- Have you replied to it already, calmly, for the next reader?
- Has anyone at the business contacted the reviewer? If so, stop; that is not the route.
- Does the review describe a visit that happened? Check quietly, and do not say what you found in public.
- Note the date, the reason and the status; use the one appeal only if the facts support it.
- If the review is unlawful rather than against policy, that is a solicitor's conversation.
