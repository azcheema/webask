Two problems, in the order they arrive. The phone rings while you are on a job and the caller books the next name on the list. And the paperwork — the quote, the cancellation notice, the certificate, the number on the van — lives in five places and matches in none of them.

Both have a fix that a website and a phone system can do for you. Both also have rules that the pages selling those fixes leave out: we read four UK text-back products in September 2026, found PECR on none of their pages, and a "book today" line or an emoji in two of the sample texts. Here are the fixes, with the rules in.

## The call you miss is the job you do not get

You cannot answer the phone from under a boiler, on a roof or with a customer in front of you. Something should. Your number — the one on the van, the site and the Google profile — stays as it is. Behind it sits a divert: when a call rings out, the caller gets a text within seconds from a number registered to your business, saying you missed them and how to reply or book, and the reply lands in one inbox on the phone in your pocket.

Three things make that a service you provide rather than marketing you never had consent for. The text carries no offer, no discount and no review link, because the ICO's test for whether a message is marketing is about content, and a message that tells someone you missed their call and how to reach you is a service message until it promotes something. It comes from a number tied to your business — the line your calls divert to, or a new UK number registered to your trading name — never to us. And the caller's number is used to reply to that call and for nothing else unless they ask.

Out of hours, the same divert can go to an automated assistant that says what it is in its first sentence, takes the job details and books the visit. It never calls anyone back; that is a different thing in law, and a different service.

## The reply that becomes a quote

Here is the rule that follows the reply, and the one a text-back cannot do for you. When a customer accepts your quote at their home — or by text or phone straight after you quoted them there — it is an off-premises contract; when a customer you have not visited accepts by phone, text, email or your website, and taking work that way is how you trade, it is a distance contract. Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 either kind gives the customer the right to cancel "at any time in the cancellation period without giving any reason", and for a service the period "ends at the end of 14 days after the day on which the contract is entered into".

Three consequences follow, and the quote flow on the site is built for all three. You must give the customer the information in Schedule 2 to the Regulations — the cancellation right among it, with a cancellation form — "in a clear and comprehensible manner" before they are bound, and confirm it on paper — or by email, if the customer agrees. If the cancellation information is the part that is missing, the period runs to "12 months after the day on which it would have ended", and a customer who cancels in that time pays nothing for the work done. And you must not start the work inside the fourteen days unless the customer "has made an express request", on paper or by email for a doorstep quote — with that request in hand, a customer who cancels part-way pays "in proportion to what has been supplied".

There is one exception worth knowing precisely: a visit the customer "has specifically requested" for "urgent repairs or maintenance" carries no right to cancel — but "services in addition to the urgent repairs or maintenance requested" do. And a doorstep job where the customer pays "not more than £42" sits outside both the information duties and the right to cancel; a quote accepted by phone, text, email or your website has no such floor.

None of this is exotic. It is a line in the quote email, a tick box that records the request to start, and a confirmation the system sends by itself.

## What your website says is part of the contract

The Consumer Rights Act 2015 treats "anything that is said or written to the consumer, by or on behalf of the trader, about the trader or the service" as a term of the contract if the customer takes it into account when deciding to go ahead. A promise on your site — same-day call-out, a ten-year guarantee, no call-out charge — binds you as if it were in the quote. The Act also puts two terms into every job whether or not you write them: the work is done "with reasonable care and skill", and where no price was fixed the customer pays "a reasonable price for the service, and no more".

So the copy is written the way the quote is written: specific, qualified where it needs to be, and true on a bad day as well as a good one.

## The register, the logo and the number on the van

Gas Safe Register's brand policy is short and worth quoting: "Only registered gas businesses are permitted to display the appropriate Gas Safe Register brand and/or a Gas Safe registration number and these can only be used in association with the registered trading name." Its definition of marketing material includes "websites, adverts, vehicle signage, business stationery, social media, online website directories", and misuse by a registered business is treated as a breach of the Rules of Registration. TrustMark permits its logo only while a business is registered, and refers persistent misuse to Trading Standards. A competent person scheme lets a registered installer self-certify that work of the kinds the scheme covers complies with the Building Regulations, so the customer needs no approval of their own and receives "a certificate within 8 weeks of completion".

On the site that means the brand or your registration number only alongside your registered trading name, laid out as the scheme's Brand Guidelines require and checked against the register on the day the page goes live; the scheme's name where the certificate comes from; and never the words approved or accredited unless the scheme's own register uses them. A claim of approval that is not true is the kind of statement consumer law treats as unfair, and one a competitor can report in a minute.

## The details every trading website must carry

A limited company's website must show its registered name, its registered number, the part of the UK it is registered in and its registered office address. Anyone trading online must show their name, a geographic address, an email address and, where registered, a VAT number, and any price must say clearly whether it includes VAT. Sole traders are not exempt from the second list. We put them in the footer and on the contact page as a matter of course, and the free audit lists which are missing from the site you have — it does not tell you a site "is compliant", because that is not a verdict we sell.

## Reviews that survive a look

Since 6 April 2025 the rules on reviews have had a regulator with the power to fine directly, and its guidance describes asking only the satisfied customers as cherry-picking. Google's own policy says merchants should not selectively solicit positive reviews or offer anything for one. The review programme therefore asks every customer, at the same point after the job, offers nothing, drafts a reply to each review for your approval and shows your rating on the site as Google shows it. It is slower than the shortcut, and it is the version you can defend.

## Your Google profile as a service-area business

Google's rule is that a business which "travels to customers where they are" is eligible for a profile and "should hide your business address from customers", showing the area served instead. Managed in your own account with us as a manager, the profile carries the services you actually offer, the hours a phone is answered, photographs of the work and the van, and the same name, number and trading details as the site, character for character — because Google's guidelines ask for the name your website uses, and a mismatch is an easy thing for a customer to spot.
