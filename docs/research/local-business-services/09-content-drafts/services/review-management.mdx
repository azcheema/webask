## Ask everyone, incentivise nobody, hide nothing

That sentence is the whole design, and every feature on this page is one of its three clauses made mechanical. Every customer gets the same request at the same point in the job, whether or not you think they were happy. Nothing is offered for a review — not a discount, not a prize draw, not a coffee. And what customers write is shown as Google shows it, on your profile and on your site, with the low scores in.

It is also the honest answer to the question people actually search: how do you get more Google reviews from customers? You ask all of them, promptly, once, with a single tap, and you reply to what comes back. A promise of more than that is a promise of a shortcut, and the two shortcuts are the ones the rules now name.

## "How to get more Google reviews from customers"

The request goes by text or email within a set time of the job or visit — an hour for a trade, a day for a salon, whatever fits the service — with one link straight to the review form. It asks for a review and nothing else: no offer, no "tell us if you're happy first", no second message to the ones who did not click. Timed per service, sent to everyone, and then left alone.

Two things make that work better than any incentive. Asking everyone samples the business as it is, so the rating you earn is one you can defend. And asking once, cleanly, keeps the request a plain request rather than a marketing message to a number you may not hold consent for — the [CRM page](/services/crm-automation) sets out who you may lawfully message, and a review request that starts promoting the business joins that list.

## What changed on 6 April 2025

The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 made it illegal in the UK to write, commission or publish fake consumer reviews, to conceal that a review was incentivised, and to present reviews in a misleading way. The CMA can now determine a breach itself and fine directly — up to 10% of global annual turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher — and in March 2026 it opened investigations into five named businesses over review handling.

Its guidance is specific about the shortcuts. Selecting who gets asked is one: "encouraging just those who are satisfied to leave reviews" is described as cherry-picking. Pausing the requests when a bad run comes in is another — "arbitrarily stopping and starting review invitations". Offering something for a review is allowed only if the review is labelled "prominently as incentivised, namely, as an advert" and still reflects the customer's genuine experience. And the plain request — "merely emailing customers generally to ask if they wish to provide a review" — is, in the CMA's words, "not prohibited under the banned practice". Everyone, no incentive, nothing hidden: the design is the guidance, read carefully.

## Why we will not run a "rate us first" flow

Some tools offer a private score first, releasing the public link only on a good one. We do not offer that. Under the CMA's guidance that is cherry-picking by another route; under Google's policy, which every Google review lives inside, merchants should not "Discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers", should not offer incentives "in exchange for posting any review or revision or removal of a negative review", and should not pressure people to review "while on the premises". A programme that gates is a programme with two problems, and a competitor can be the first to notice.

When we read six review platforms sold in the UK in September 2026, one pricing page mentioned the Act — a line about labelling reviews in line with it — and none set out what the Act says about incentives or gating. The rules are not new; five of the six pages we read did not mention them.

## Replies, drafted for your approval

Every review gets a reply, because an unanswered review is a customer talking to an empty room, and an unanswered bad one is a story with only one side. We draft them — short, specific, in your voice, thanking the good ones and taking the bad ones seriously without arguing — and nothing is posted in your name until you have approved it. A reply that disputes a customer's account in public is a reply we will not draft.

## The widget shows what Google shows

If you want reviews on your own site, the widget pulls them from your Google profile as they are: the rating Google calculates, the reviews Google has published, newest first. No filtering to four stars and above, no hand-picked wall, no hiding the one from March. Presenting reviews in a misleading way is one of the practices the Act names, and a wall of only your best ones is the textbook case.

## We are inside the rules too

The CMA's guidance names "marketing companies, individuals acting on behalf of traders" among those who can engage in the banned practice by commissioning fake or concealed reviews. We run the requests, so the rules run through us. That is not a disclaimer; it is the reason the programme is built this way and the reason we will decline the incentive when you ask for it. What you are buying is a review programme built to the regulator's own guidance, run by someone bound by the same guidance.

## Dentists: the GDC layer

For dental practices the General Dental Council adds its own rule on reviews; the [dental practices](/industries/dental-practices) page sets it out, and the programme is built to meet it because it never offers anything to anyone. Aesthetic clinics have a different question, about what a published review may name, and until that is settled the site widget stays off for them and reviews are shown on Google only.

## What the monthly report shows

Requests sent. Reviews received. Rating movement. Reviews still unanswered. Four counts from your Google profile and the account that runs it, which you can log into — nothing modelled, no percentage we did not measure. If requests are going out and reviews are not coming in, the timing is wrong for that service, and the report is how we find out.

## Two limits, stated up front

We do not remove or suppress genuine negative reviews, and we do not write reviews. Writing reviews has been a banned practice under the Act since 6 April 2025; suppressing genuine ones is what the CMA's guidance says may amount to misleading presentation; no plan on this site includes either. A review that breaks Google's own rules — spam, a competitor's fake, an off-topic rant — can be reported to Google through the normal process, and we will do that; whether it comes down is Google's decision.

Nor is this crisis or press reputation work. If the problem is a news story rather than a customer, you need a different kind of help.
