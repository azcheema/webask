## What happens in the first minute after a missed call

You are under a boiler, in a treatment room or halfway up a ladder. The phone rings out. Within seconds the caller has a text from a number tied to your business — the one they dialled, or one they will recognise: sorry we missed you, reply here, or book. Their reply lands in one inbox, on a phone and on a screen, where whoever is free answers it.

That is the whole product. The interesting part is not the automation — any platform can send a text when a call is missed — but three decisions underneath it: what the message says, which number it comes from, and what happens to the caller's number afterwards. Get those three right and the text is a service you provide. Get them wrong and it is marketing you never had consent to send.

## The text we send, word for word

> Hi, it's {Business}. Sorry we missed your call. Reply here and we'll get back to you, or book at {short link}. Reply STOP to opt out.

Five things about that message are deliberate. It names who sent it. It says why it was sent. It gives the caller a way to reply and a way to book, and it gives them a way to stop. It carries no offer — no discount, no "book today", no review link — and at 147 characters with a 24-character business name and a twelve-character link placeholder it fits inside one 160-character text, so it arrives as one message and costs one message.

The wording is yours to adjust, within those five constraints. The constraints are not house style; they are what keeps the next section true.

## "Why do I get a text message after a missed call?" — the caller's side

Because you told them something they needed: that you saw the call, and how to reach you. The ICO, which enforces the UK's marketing rules, describes messages "for administrative or customer services purposes" — reminding people how to contact you, confirming appointments — as service messages rather than direct marketing. Its test is about content: "if your service message has elements that are direct marketing, even if that is not the main purpose of your message, then it will count as direct marketing", while "general branding or logos" do not.

So the text-back stays a service message for exactly as long as it says nothing promotional. The moment a template gains "20% off this week", it is an advert, sent to a number you hold no marketing consent for. That is why the offer is the one thing we will not add, however good the offer.

The caller can reply, and the reply goes to a person. If they say stop, it stops — and their number is not added to anything. It is used to reply to that call, and for nothing else unless they ask.

## Is an automatic text back allowed in the UK?

The rules that matter are the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations. Under PECR a text message is "electronic mail", so the email rules apply to texts. Regulation 22 restricts unsolicited **marketing** by electronic mail to "individual subscribers" — sole traders, partnerships and people on personal numbers — unless they have consented, or the narrow soft opt-in applies: details obtained "in the course of the sale or negotiations for the sale", marketing of "similar products and services only", and "a simple means of refusing" offered at collection and in every message since. Limited companies, LLPs and public bodies are corporate subscribers, and can be texted without prior consent.

Two honest points follow. First, none of that is needed for a service message that carries no marketing, which is the design here. Second, whether a missed call counts as "negotiations for the sale" is a question nobody has tested, and we do not build on it: the text-back does not rely on soft opt-in, and it does not enrol the caller in anything that would. If you later want to market to people who called you, that is the [Email & SMS Marketing](/services/email-sms-marketing) plan, and it begins with the consent question, not with the list.

The ceiling for getting this wrong is real — PECR fines run to £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, since 20 August 2025 — and, for a small business, beside the point. The realistic cost is a complaint, a letter from the ICO and a list of callers who replied STOP and may not ring again. The cheaper route is a message that never needs defending.

The full argument on who you may lawfully message, by legal form, is on our [CRM page](/services/crm-automation). This page does not restate it; it applies it.

## Your number, or a new one registered to your business

The text comes from the number the caller dialled, or from one they will recognise. Two ways to do that.

**A divert from the number you publish.** The number on your van, your website and your Google profile stays exactly as it is; unanswered calls forward to a line the platform watches, and the text goes out from a number tied to your business. Nothing on your marketing changes, and if you ever leave, nothing has to change back.

**A new UK number, registered to you.** On the platform we use, a UK long-code number sits on an approved regulatory compliance bundle (the platform's UK guidance, July 2024): the business name, its registration and a UK business address go on the record, and a Companies House number means no further documents. A business not registered in the UK gets a UK mobile or freephone number instead. Either way the number is registered to your business, in your account on the platform, not to us. Branded sender names — the ones that show a name instead of a number — only work one way, so a text-back that expects a reply always comes from a real number.

We recommend the divert unless you want a second line for a reason.

## One inbox: replies, web chat and WhatsApp

Replies to the text arrive in the same inbox as your website chat and, if you switch it on, WhatsApp. Whoever is free answers from the app; the conversation history stays with the contact, so the second person to pick it up can see what the first said.

WhatsApp is an opt-in channel: a customer has to give you the number and agree to messages on it before you send anything there, and the platform bills each WhatsApp message separately. Web chat has neither constraint. The text-back itself needs neither, because it answers a call the customer made.

## What the monthly report shows

Calls missed. Texts sent. Conversations where the caller replied. Bookings that followed a reply. Four counts from the account that runs it, which you can log into, with nothing modelled and no percentage we did not measure. If the numbers say the divert is catching calls you would rather answer live, the report is how you find out, and changing the divert is a setting, not a project.

## Two limits, stated up front

This plan does not send promotional follow-up to the people who called you. It could — the platform makes it one click — and that click is where a service message becomes marketing to a number without consent. Follow-up that promotes is a different plan, built on a lawful basis first.

Nor does it answer the call. If the phone should be picked up by something rather than texted back, that is the [AI Receptionist](/services/ai-receptionist) plan: a narrow inbound agent on the same divert, disclosed as automated in its first sentence. The two work together, and the text-back is the fallback when the agent hands over.
