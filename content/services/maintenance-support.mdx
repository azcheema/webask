## Two things decay after launch, not one

Everyone selling a care plan tells you about the first: code ages, and someone has to keep it
current. True, necessary, and priced into every plan here.

The second is the one almost nobody sells against, and in the UK it is the expensive one.

**Your website was compliant on the day it launched. The rules it complies with keep moving.**
Nothing on the site changes. No file is edited, no error reaches a log. The page simply drifts out
of line with a position that shifted underneath it — and unlike a broken form, this failure mode
has a regulator attached and no error message.

That is what a UK care plan is actually for. The patching is table stakes; the re-check is the part
worth paying for.

## What upkeep actually costs in the UK

Numbers before argument. Two published UK benchmarks bracket this, and they measure different
things:

| Benchmark                                       | UK figure, 2026                  | What it covers                               |
| ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Domain and hosting on a typical freelance build | **£100–£300 a year**             | Keeping the lights on. Renewals and a server |
| Genuine all-in ongoing cost                     | **15–20% of build cost, a year** | Keeping the thing working                    |
| Which, on a £3,500 build, is                    | **roughly £525–£700 a year**     | Arithmetic, not a quote                      |

Read those honestly and two things follow. £100–£300 a year is not maintenance — it is a hosting
bill wearing maintenance's coat. And a simple brochure site has a real upkeep number in the
hundreds, not the thousands.

Neither prices the second decay curve. That is the gap this page is about.

## The rules move. Your pages do not.

Four live UK examples, all current as at **28 July 2026**, each of which changes what a site should
say without anyone touching the site.

**Reviews and testimonials — DMCC Act 2024, in force 6 April 2025.** Writing, commissioning or
publishing fake consumer reviews is now illegal in the UK, as is concealing an incentive behind a
genuine one. The CMA can determine a breach and fine directly, without going to court — up to
**10% of global annual turnover or £300,000**, whichever is higher, and **£150,000** for an
individual. In **March 2026** it opened investigations into five named businesses over review
handling.

**Cookie banners — PECR.** Consent has to be freely given, which in the ICO's reading means
"reject all" carries the same visual weight as "accept all", and nothing non-essential fires
before a choice is made. The ICO wrote to **53 of the UK's top 100 websites** about this, and
exposure runs to **£17.5m or 4% of global turnover**. Banners rarely break on their own — they
break when someone adds a tag or embeds a video.

**Aesthetics licensing — not yet in force.** As at **July 2026** the England licensing scheme for
non-surgical cosmetic procedures is **not yet operative**. The Government published its
consultation response in **August 2025** — 11,848 responses — proposing a **red, amber and green**
risk categorisation, and stated an intention to consult on the highest-risk procedures in
**spring 2026**. Any clinic page that states the position with confidence is going to be wrong on a
date nobody has diarised.

**Private dentistry — CMA market study, opened March 2026.** A study into the **£8.4bn** private
dentistry sector, with pricing transparency squarely in scope. A price list that was reasonable in
2024 is now worth re-reading on a schedule.

One rule follows from all four, and it binds this page as tightly as it binds any site on a plan:

> **Stale regulatory content is worse than none.** A page that confidently states last year's
> position does not merely fail to help — it inverts the trust it was written to build. It is also
> the one kind of content found without a complainant: the ASA now runs AI-powered proactive
> monitoring and does not wait to be told.

## Accessibility is a claim with an expiry date

WCAG 2.2 AA conformance describes a site on a given day, not a permanent property of it. It
regresses through ordinary use: an image uploaded without alternative text, a colour tweaked for a
campaign, a third-party booking widget dropped into a page.

In the UK the framing is the **Equality Act 2010** — service providers must make reasonable
adjustments, and an inaccessible website is a recognised risk area. The sensible response is not
an annual audit certifying a moment in time but a gate on every deploy: an automated pass in
continuous integration, axe-core in the test suite, and a manual keyboard run when structure
changes. That is the standard this site is built to, and the one a plan holds a site to.

## What actually goes into the quarterly re-check

The routine half of a plan is set out in the panel on this page and holds no surprises. What
separates a UK plan from a generic support retainer is the review — specifically, what gets read
during it, because the things that go stale are not the things that break.

- **The consent banner, in the network tab rather than the code.** Whether a non-essential cookie
  fires before consent is a question about what the browser did, not what the configuration says it
  should have done.
- **Reviews and testimonials** — still real, still named, still consented, any incentive still
  disclosed.
- **Dated regulatory statements** — every claim about a rule carries a date, and the date is what
  we come back to. Where a position has moved, the page moves with it.
- **Published prices and terms**, in sectors where transparency is under active scrutiny.
- **The accessibility gate**, which either passes or does not, and the layout-shift and
  main-thread budgets alongside it.

None of it is automatable. A tool can tell you a dependency is out of date; it cannot tell you that
a sentence written in good faith in 2024 now describes a rule that has since changed. How
engagements run is set out on [our process](/process).

## When a plan is the wrong purchase

The benchmark cuts both ways. **If your site is five static pages with no forms, no reviews, no
bookings and no claim a regulator has an opinion about, the £100–£300 figure is right and we are
the wrong purchase.** Renew the domain, pay for decent hosting, and put the difference somewhere it
earns.

What justifies the spend is not page count but consequence. The useful question is what a bad week
costs you: a booking flow that stops taking bookings, a checkout that drops payments, a price list
that misstates what you charge. Where the answer is measured in money, or in a letter from a
regulator, the plan is cheaper than the week. Where it is not, keep your money.

Some businesses cross that line the day they launch. An
[aesthetic clinic](/industries/aesthetic-clinics) or a
[dental practice](/industries/dental-practices) publishes claim-bearing, consent-bearing,
price-bearing content on its very first page, under regulators currently in motion. An e-commerce
build crosses it the moment it takes a card.

## Inherited builds, and WordPress in particular

Most UK small-business sites run on WordPress, and an inherited one is a different maintenance
object to a site built from scratch. Most of the code you become responsible for is code you cannot
read: plugins on their own release cadences, a theme that may no longer be supported, and embeds
that set cookies without anyone having decided they should. That last is the specifically UK
problem: PECR asks what the browser actually did, and a plugin installed in 2022 can answer that on
your behalf.

The surprise on an inherited site is often not a security hole at all. It is content nobody has
read in three years: a price list that predates the current one, a policy page naming a process
that no longer happens, and testimonials that arrived with the theme rather than from a customer.
That last category stopped being merely embarrassing in April 2025.

We can point at one without leaving the room. The 2020-era WordPress site this rebuild replaces
carries exactly that today: placeholder testimonials, and a staff page for a business that has
never had staff. It is the reason removing them is a launch gate here rather than a tidy-up.
Reading an inherited site before agreeing to maintain it is not diligence theatre; it is the only
way to know what you have taken on.

## What a care plan is not

**We are not solicitors, and a care plan is not a legal opinion.** We read the rules properly,
build and maintain to them, and where a position is unsettled we say so on the page and date it
rather than sounding confident. Where copy sits close to the line, CAP's Copy Advice service exists
for precisely that — free, non-binding, and better than an opinion — and using it is part of the
method, not an escalation.

An agency that presents its regulatory reading as legal certainty has told you something about how
carefully it reads. And the reason a scheduled re-check exists at all is that the ground will not
hold still: as at 28 July 2026 the England licensing scheme is still pre-commencement and the CMA's
dentistry study is still open. Both will have moved before a year of upkeep is out.

## Four questions worth asking any UK agency

Including this one. If a care plan cannot survive them, it is a subscription rather than a service.

- **Where do the monitoring alerts go?** If the honest answer is "you'll notice the site is down",
  that is not monitoring.
- **Who reads the pages, and on what schedule?** Patching can be automated. Reading cannot — and
  the UK exposure sits in the reading.
- **Whose name is on the accounts?** Hosting, domain, analytics and CRM should be yours throughout,
  so leaving costs you a notice period and nothing else.
- **Will you tell me when to stop paying you?** A plan sold to a site that does not need one is the
  easiest recurring revenue in this industry.
