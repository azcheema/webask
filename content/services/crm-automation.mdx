## The rule most UK CRM builds get wrong

A CRM built to a US playbook assumes you can email any business address you have collected. In the UK that assumption is wrong often enough to matter — and a CRM is where a wrong assumption stops being an opinion and starts sending on a schedule.

PECR — the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations — decides whether a marketing email needs prior consent, and it decides it by the **legal form of the recipient**, not by whether the address looks like a work address.

| Recipient                                                    | Prior consent needed?              |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Limited company, LLP or public body — a corporate subscriber | **No**                             |
| Sole trader                                                  | **Yes** — treated as an individual |
| Unincorporated partnership                                   | **Yes** — treated as an individual |
| Any freemail address (`gmail.com` and the like)              | **Yes**, whoever uses it for work  |

There is one narrow exemption — **soft opt-in** — and it is narrower than most people assume. It applies only where the details came from a sale or negotiations for a sale, you are marketing your own similar services, and a clear opt-out was given at collection **and** in every message since. Bought lists and scraped addresses never qualify, whatever the vendor selling them says.

Now hold that against the shape of the UK market. SMEs are **99.85%** of UK businesses, and that base skews heavily towards sole traders and micro-companies. "We can email any business" is a US-shaped assumption that quietly imports a compliance problem into a British pipeline, and once wired into an automation it runs at volume, unattended.

PECR exposure runs to **£17.5m or 4% of global turnover, whichever is higher**, though a six-person practice should not plan around the ceiling — the realistic cost is a complaint, an ICO letter and a wrecked sending reputation. The point is the asymmetry: at build time this costs a field and a filter; retrofitted later, considerably more.

## Segmenting by legal form is a build step, not a policy

Policies get written down and forgotten; fields and filters keep working. So this belongs in the build as configuration, not in an induction pack as a paragraph:

- **A legal-form value on the contact record**, set at capture rather than guessed later — corporate subscribers on one side, sole traders, unincorporated partnerships and freemail addresses on the other.
- **Freemail treated as an individual at the point of entry.** A `gmail.com` address typed into a field labelled "company email" is still an individual under PECR, and the form should decide that, not a human triaging a spreadsheet later.
- **Consent provenance stored rather than implied** — where a record came from a sale or a live negotiation, with a date and a source. That is the only thing making a soft opt-in claim defensible afterwards.
- **Sequences that check before they send.** Automations filter on lawful basis at entry, and anything without one lands in a manual queue rather than in a send.

That is the difference between a CRM that scales your outreach and one that scales your exposure.

## What a migration actually loses

Not the contacts. The consent.

A CSV export carries the email address. It does not carry the lawful basis, the date an opt-out was offered, the source of the record, or which of your previous three tools that person already unsubscribed from. Import it clean and you have not moved a list, you have laundered one — and now it runs on automation.

So the consent model is rebuilt before any sequence runs — legal form, lawful basis, opt-out history, source — and where the old system cannot answer, the record arrives unconsented rather than optimistically tagged.

Be ready for what that does to the number. A contact count and a mailable list are different figures, and an honest migration is where you learn by how much. That is not a loss — it is the first accurate view of the list, and far cheaper to reach in a scoped migration than in a complaint.

## Automations that touch regulated clients

A CRM stops being neutral the moment your customers are patients. Four places the default configuration creates a problem:

**Review requests.** Where a platform ships one, it is the easiest way to automate yourself into a legal problem. Since **6 April 2025** the DMCC Act 2024 has made publishing fake consumer reviews illegal in the UK, and incentivised reviews must be disclosed — an incentive being a discount or a freebie, not just cash. The CMA can determine a breach and fine directly, up to **10% of global annual turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher**; in **March 2026** it opened investigations into five named businesses. Dental practices carry a second layer: the GDC requires genuine reviews with no incentives at all. So the automation asks everyone, incentivises nobody, and does not quietly route the unhappy ones away from the request.

**A clinic's nurture sequence is advertising.** The CAP Code applies to organic content, not only paid ads, so an automated email or SMS is an ad. Prescription-only medicines may not be advertised to the public, and the ASA treats euphemisms as implied promotion of the medicine — which rules out a rebooking reminder that names the product, and the softer wording a practice reaches for once it knows it cannot. The MHRA issued **47 enforcement notices to aesthetic businesses in 2024**, mostly on exactly this. The compliant sequence promotes the consultation, and that constraint belongs inside the automation: see [websites for UK aesthetic clinics](/industries/aesthetic-clinics).

**Recall is not marketing, until somebody merges them.** In a dental practice the recall reminder and the promotional offer travel down the same pipeline unless the build separates them. PECR governs both recall and marketing communications, so they are built as two flows with their own consent state, not one list with a hopeful tag on it. See [websites for UK dental practices](/industries/dental-practices).

**Anything that dials.** Outbound marketing calls placed by automated calling systems need the subscriber's prior explicit consent under PECR, and Ofcom's persistent-misuse rules add abandoned-call caps and permitted calling hours. Inbound automation — reception, booking, out-of-hours capture — is a far cleaner place to start. Whether an AI-voiced call counts as "live" or "automated" under PECR is a grey zone the regulator has not formally resolved, and an unresolved classification is a poor thing to discover once the dialler is already running.
