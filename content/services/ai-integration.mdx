An AI voice agent answering your phone in the UK is not only a technology decision. It sits under **PECR** and **UK GDPR**, policed by the ICO, and under **Ofcom's** nuisance-call rules. That regulatory picture goes first on this page rather than last, because it changes what gets built — and because a well-advised buyer will raise it anyway.

Starting at **£4,500 + VAT where applicable**. Voice agents first, since that is where the law bites, then chatbots and workflow automation, where it mostly does not.

## The part of UK law that is genuinely unresolved

PECR divides telephone calls into two kinds: **live** calls — a human, or something human-equivalent — and **automated** calls, meaning recorded or pre-recorded messages. Different rules attach to each. An AI voice agent is neither, cleanly.

The regulator has not formally resolved that grey zone. Informal ICO guidance points one way: an AI capable of genuine two-way conversation, with a human handoff available, looks more like a live call than a recorded message. That is an indication, not a ruling.

So: unsettled, with a lean. Anyone telling you the classification is decided is either guessing or selling.

We build for the safer reading rather than the convenient one — genuine two-way conversation instead of a decision tree with a voice on top, a real route to a person, and identification at the start of the call rather than an agent that lets callers assume it is human. If the position hardens, a system built that way does not need rebuilding.

## Voice agents, built inbound-first

Inbound is the clean case, and the right place for a voice engagement to start: reception, booking, out-of-hours capture, the overflow when three lines ring at once. The caller rang you. Nothing is being marketed at them, so the consent question never arises.

The agent's job is narrow on purpose, and the reason is testability rather than modesty: an agent permitted to hold any conversation is one nobody can evaluate, while an agent with a defined job can be run against a fixed set of cases before it ever picks up. Judge it on its failure mode rather than its success rate — one that handles two-thirds of calls and hands the rest cleanly to a person beats one that handles nine in ten and improvises through the last, because the improvised tenth is a wrong answer given to a real customer under your name.

Clinics and practices are the clearest inbound case: enquiries arrive by phone, often outside opening hours, and the caller wants a consultation booked rather than a voicemail beep.

**Call recording is a decision to take before launch, not after.** Callers must be told, up front. RIPA permits recording without consent in some circumstances, but UK GDPR transparency pulls the other way, and the ICO's position is that informing callers supports the fairness principle even where consent is not strictly required. Silence is not consent. In practice that means the disclosure sits in the agent's opening line, the retention period is chosen deliberately, and the recordings land somewhere you own.

## Outbound is a different regime, and it is scoped separately

This is where the answer stops being comfortable.

- **Consent.** Outbound marketing calls made using automated calling systems require the subscriber's **prior explicit consent** under PECR. Not soft opt-in, not a bought list, not "they are a business, so it is fine".
- **Ofcom's persistent-misuse rules** govern the calling operation itself: caps on abandoned-call rates, permitted calling hours, and requirements for what an abandoned-call message has to say.
- **CLI authenticity and number allocation.** The 2025 revision to **General Condition 6** hardened both, with carrier-level filtering where they are not met — so non-compliant traffic stops connecting, which is a commercial problem before it is a legal one.

Two regulators reaching different parts of the same campaign, then. A PECR breach carries exposure of **£17.5m or 4% of global turnover, whichever is higher** at the ICO; Ofcom can act on how the calling is conducted whether or not anyone consented to it.

Our position: we will build outbound, but only against a consent record you can evidence, inside permitted hours, on authentic CLI, with abandoned-call behaviour configured rather than defaulted. Where that record does not exist, the project does not begin with a dialler — it begins with the inbound agent, which captures demand you already have and is not waiting on a legal question.

None of this is legal advice — it is our reading as at July 2026. Where a situation sits close to a line, we say so and cite the source.

## What the model is allowed to say

Everything above concerns how a call is placed. This concerns what comes out of the model once it connects, and in a regulated sector that is the harder question.

The CAP Code applies to organic content, not only to paid advertising, so an answer your agent gives a member of the public is your own marketing copy. It is also the only copy on your site that gets written after publication, by something that has never read your compliance notes.

For an aesthetic clinic that is not theoretical. Prescription-only medicines may not be advertised to the public, and the ASA reads a euphemism as a reference to the product itself — so the model breaches precisely when it is being most helpful, by answering in the words the caller used. MHRA enforcement notices to aesthetic businesses ran to **47 in 2024**, mostly on this rule, and the ASA's proactive monitoring is itself AI-powered: it finds non-compliant material without waiting for a complaint. Dentistry has its own version. The GDC treats "specialist" as a protected description tied to its specialist list, so an assistant that helpfully agrees a practice specialises in something can create a standards breach in a single sentence.

The build answer is constraint rather than supervision after the fact. The retrieval corpus is copy that has already been through a compliance review, the agent declines rather than improvises on treatment questions, and anything that would need a product named is a handover to a person. The terminology rules a clinic site lives under are set out on [websites for UK aesthetic clinics](/industries/aesthetic-clinics), and the dental equivalents on [websites for UK dental practices](/industries/dental-practices).

## A chatbot's first UK problem is the widget, not the model

A chat widget that sets non-essential storage before the visitor has consented is a PECR problem however good the model behind it is, and "we set it on page load and asked a moment later" is not a defence. So nothing non-essential fires before consent, reject carries the same visual prominence as accept — the ICO's main enforcement focus, and the commonest failure on UK sites — and the widget waits behind the same gate as analytics instead of being quietly exempted from it as "functional".

Past the gate, the question is whether the answers hold up over time. Grounding settles day one; it does nothing about day ninety. Providers update their models on their own schedule and your prices and policies change on yours; either can move an answer with nobody touching the build. So the questions your business genuinely receives — the awkward ones, the price ones, the ones asked before anyone will book — become a fixed test set, run before launch and after every change, which makes a regression something you detect rather than something a customer reports. Where a conversation qualifies, it belongs in the pipeline rather than the chat log; [CRM automation](/services/crm-automation) is the receiving end.

## Workflow automation, and why its mistakes differ in kind

The third category never demos well and is usually the first to pay for itself: the admin that never appears on an invoice. What makes a task a candidate is not how tedious it is but how decidable it is. If two competent people would produce the same output from the same inputs, a workflow can do it; where they would disagree, a model in the middle is guessing and you have automated the disagreement. We build these on n8n or Make, and that same test decides where a model belongs and where a plain rule is cheaper — a workflow calling an LLM to do arithmetic is a slower, dearer, less reliable spreadsheet.

The failure mode worth designing for is scale. A person who emails the wrong list makes the mistake once and notices by lunchtime; an automation makes it several thousand times before anyone opens the bounce report. Where the messages are marketing email, PECR decides lawfulness by the legal form of each recipient — not something a workflow can infer from a domain name. That segmentation belongs upstream in the system holding the contact record, and how it gets built is set out on [CRM automation](/services/crm-automation). What belongs here is narrower: a workflow able to send checks the lawful basis on each record as it sends, and parks whatever cannot answer.

## Start with the leak, not the technology

The useful first question is not which of the three you want. It is where the business is losing something you could measure — calls that ring out, enquiries that arrive at 9pm and are cold by morning, an afternoon a week moving the same data between two systems. Name the leak and the right build is usually obvious; start from the technology and you get a demo.

Often the leak closes with no model in it at all — a better booking page, a shorter form, a rule the workflow can follow on its own. Those cost less and break in fewer places, and where that is the true answer it is the one you will get.
