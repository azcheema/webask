## The day your data policy stops being a document

Most UK businesses hold their data protection position in three files: a privacy notice, a retention schedule, and a record of processing activities. Nothing in the running system enforces any of them.

Retention is a sentence in a document; the data sits in a spreadsheet until somebody deletes the spreadsheet. Access control is a promise; everybody with the shared login sees everything. Consent is a tick box whose answer was never stored anywhere you can query.

A bespoke application is where those documents become schema, code and scheduled jobs — or where it becomes obvious they never will. That is the real case for custom software development in the UK, and a sharper one than "the spreadsheet has got too big".

## The UK GDPR decisions that become database columns

Six things move from policy into the build, and all six are settled during Discover, not in the week before launch.

| Policy decision        | What it becomes in the build                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Lawful basis**       | A stored value per record and per purpose. Where it is consent, the timestamp and the exact wording shown are stored with it      |
| **Data minimisation**  | Fields you cannot justify never get created. The cheapest way to protect a column is not to have one                              |
| **Retention**          | A scheduled job that genuinely deletes or anonymises on the schedule you published, and logs what it removed                      |
| **Access control**     | Roles enforced server-side on the query, not by hiding a menu item. One place to check, so every route checks                     |
| **Audit logging**      | An append-only record of who saw and changed what, and when — written from day one, because history cannot be reconstructed later |
| **Access and erasure** | An export path and a deletion path one person can run in minutes, not a fortnight of manual work                                  |

None of that is exotic engineering. What makes it expensive is doing it second. Retention retrofitted has to run against records collected under a basis nobody wrote down, so the job cannot tell what it may delete. Erasure retrofitted means opening every table that mentions a person, by hand, against a deadline you did not set. Consent retrofitted is a column of nulls and an educated guess. Settled early, each of the six is a decision; settled late, each is a project.

## Who is actually accountable, and why buying does not change it

Buying software does not move the obligation; it only moves the code. Whoever decided to collect the data still answers for it, whether it sits in a bespoke application, a subscription product, or a no-code table somebody set up on a Tuesday.

That principle grows a sharp edge as soon as a business operates across a border. Article 27 of the UK GDPR requires an organisation with no UK office, branch or other establishment, which processes UK residents' personal data in connection with offering goods or services to them or monitoring their behaviour in the UK, to appoint a UK representative and to name that representative in its privacy notice. The test is establishment — not domain name, not where the servers sit — which is why it catches organisations that assume it cannot: an overseas parent selling in, a remote-first company with no branch here, a platform whose only UK footprint is its users.

WebAsk is the UK operation of a Swedish business and fully remote, so this is a rule we have had to work through about ourselves rather than read about. Where it lands for us belongs in our privacy notice.

The same test probably cuts the other way on the ICO's data protection fee, which also turns on UK establishment: £52 at the micro tier, £78 for an SME, £3,763 above that, £5 less by direct debit, and up to £4,350 for not paying when you should. One fact, two obligations, opposite answers — precisely the pair a downloaded policy template gets wrong.

## Booking systems and patient portals carry the regulator into the build

For most businesses the six rows above are the whole story. In regulated sectors they are the starting line.

**Dental.** A private practice answers to four bodies at once: the GDC on professional conduct, the CQC on registration, the ASA on advertising claims, and the CMA, which opened a market study into the £8.4bn private dentistry sector in March 2026. Each has a build consequence, not just a copy consequence.

- **Price lists rendered from one structured source**, so the published figure cannot drift from the charged figure while a market study runs.
- **Image consent captured per patient, per image and per use, and revocable.** The GDC requires patient consent for before-and-after images, which makes consent a record the portal holds, not an email somebody remembers.
- **A reviews mechanism that survives two regimes at once.** The GDC permits genuine reviews only, with no incentives. Since 6 April 2025 the DMCC Act 2024 has made publishing fake reviews, or concealing incentivised ones, illegal — with the CMA able to determine a breach and fine directly, up to 10% of global annual turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher.
- **"Specialist" as a controlled term**, because using it without the GDC specialist list behind it is a standards breach, and a free-text field invites it.
- **Recall messaging built to PECR's soft opt-in**, which only holds where the details came from a sale or its negotiation, where you are marketing your own similar services, and where a clear opt-out was offered at collection and appears in every message. PECR exposure runs to £17.5m or 4% of global turnover.

More on [dental practices](/industries/dental-practices).

**Aesthetic clinics.** The constraint here is structural, not editorial. CAP Code rule 12.12 bans advertising prescription-only medicines to the public, and the ASA treats indirect and euphemistic references as promotion of the medicine just the same. What is permitted is advertising the consultation. A price list naming a prescription-only medicine is allowed only where it sits at least two clicks from the homepage, is reached through consultation pages, stays purely informational, and appears in neither the homepage nor the primary navigation.

Read as an engineer, that stops being a copywriting rule. It is a routing rule, a navigation rule and a sitemap rule, and it belongs to whoever builds the booking flow — a system that helpfully surfaces "popular treatments" on its landing screen has undone the arrangement. Eligibility works the same way: in England it has been a criminal offence since 1 October 2021 to administer the relevant injectable procedures to under-18s for a cosmetic purpose, which puts age checking in form validation rather than in the small print.

The licensing scheme for non-surgical cosmetic procedures is **not yet in force as at July 2026** — the Government published its consultation response in August 2025, proposing a red / amber / green risk categorisation. Practitioner records that can already hold licence status, qualifications and indemnity will not need retrofitting under a deadline. More on [aesthetic clinics](/industries/aesthetic-clinics).

## Accessibility is an Equality Act duty, and internal tools are where it bites

Every build is to WCAG 2.2 AA, with checks enforced in CI rather than audited once at the end. On a public website the argument is reach. On an internal tool it is the Equality Act 2010, which requires service providers to make reasonable adjustments.

The difference is sharper than it looks. Someone who cannot use your marketing site goes elsewhere. Someone who cannot use the rota, the intake form or the approvals queue cannot do the job you hired them for. A keyboard trap in an internal tool is not a bug report; it is an employment problem with a paper trail. It is also where no-code hands you somebody else's liability: you inherit the platform's components, and you cannot fix what you cannot edit.

## Bespoke, off-the-shelf or no-code — the comparison that matters here

|                        | Bespoke build                          | Off-the-shelf SaaS                   | No-code platform                     |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Who answers to the ICO | You — and the system can show it       | You. The vendor processes for you    | You. The platform carries none of it |
| Retention              | A job you can inspect                  | Whatever the vendor's settings allow | Usually manual, usually forgotten    |
| Access control         | Roles you define, checked on the query | Their permission model, as sold      | Coarse, and often all-or-nothing     |
| Audit trail            | Append-only, yours to query            | If your plan includes it             | Rarely available                     |
| Regulated structure    | Built to the rule                      | Only if it happens to fit            | Fights you                           |

Read that as a due-diligence checklist rather than an argument for building: most of it can be settled from a vendor's documentation before you sign. Can you set the retention period, or only accept the default? Can you export the audit trail, or only look at it? Is there a published accessibility conformance statement, and what is its date? Who is named as the contact if the ICO writes about data held in that system? A product that answers all four has saved you the cost of a build.

Where one cannot, bespoke is still not automatic; the question is whether the gap matters for the data you hold. A rota for six people in a shop needs no append-only audit table. A portal holding clinical images, consent records and a recall list does, and configuration will not conjure one.

## Where a build decision stops and a legal one starts

**We are not your data protection officer, and none of this is legal advice.** Your solicitor or DPO decides the lawful basis, the retention periods and the wording of the notice. We build to that decision without softening it in implementation, and say so when it does not survive contact with the schema — a retention rule that cannot be written as a query is usually one nobody has finished writing.

**Regulated copy does not get an opinion, ours included.** Where clinic or practice wording sits close to the CAP line, it goes to CAP's free Copy Advice service before publication: slower than a confident answer, and far cheaper than an upheld ruling against the business whose name is on the page.
