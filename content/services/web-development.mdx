## What a UK small-business website actually costs

There is no market price for "a website". Across the UK agency tier the published band runs **£2,500–£10,000** for a standard small-business site, and London is dearer than the rest of the country for the same specification — a location premium rather than a scope premium. Below that tier the numbers fall away steeply, and the product changes as they do.

Four routes, genuinely different from one another rather than one product at four prices.

| Route              | Typical UK cost                                                                   | Ongoing                                 | Sensible when                                    |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| DIY site builder   | £240–£360 a year, all in                                                          | included in the subscription            | you are still testing whether the business works |
| Freelancer         | £800–£3,000; a typical four- or five-page build lands at £1,200–£2,000            | £100–£300 a year for domain and hosting | the site is a brochure that rarely changes       |
| Regional UK agency | £3,000–£6,000 for 5–15 pages with design, a responsive build, basic SEO and a CMS | 15–20% of build cost a year             | the site is one of the ways you get customers    |
| WebAsk             | from £3,500                                                                       | optional care plan from £250 a month    | the site is load-bearing and has to rank         |

**Most UK small businesses end up spending between £1,500 and £5,000 on a build.** If the quotes in front of you sit outside that, the useful question is which of those four rows each one is really pricing. Most disagreements about price turn out to be disagreements about the product, conducted in identical vocabulary.

The spread is structural rather than mysterious. Industry reporting of IBISWorld figures puts the UK web design services market at **£658.2m in 2026**, across roughly **2,206 businesses** counted in 2025 — on the order of **£300,000** of revenue per operator. A market with no dominant national player, no standard product and no shared definitions is precisely the condition in which one brief attracts wildly different numbers.

## Why the floor sits at £3,500

The figure on the card sits deliberately above the freelancer band and at the bottom of the regional agency band. That is a positioning decision, and it is more useful to explain it than to hide it.

A starting price is not an opening bid waiting to be negotiated down. It is the point below which we would have to change the _product_ rather than the effort: a template instead of a design, a shorter site, or a performance and accessibility standard checked once on launch day and never again. The price card states what the number covers. What a card cannot show is the difference between a standard measured continuously and one asserted in a proposal — and that difference is most of what separates the rows in the table.

The performance half of that floor earns its place for a specifically British reason. Google holds around **92.7% of UK search** (Q1 2025), a higher share than it holds in the United States. That makes Google's measurement of your site something other than one opinion among several: it is the only measurement that decides whether a stranger ever finds you. A slow mobile page is therefore not a question of taste. It is a number one company uses to rank you, in a market where that company is effectively the entire channel.

The quote is fixed before work starts rather than estimated hourly and revised upward as the project reveals itself. How an enquiry becomes that number is set out in [our process](/process).

## The number nobody quotes you: year two

Across the UK market, running a website costs **15–20% of its build cost per year**. That figure is missing from almost every comparison put in front of a small business, and adding it back reorders the table above.

On a freelance build the visible ongoing cost is genuinely small — **£100–£300 a year** covers the domain and hosting, and if nothing ever changes, that is all you pay. The cost that never appears on any invoice is the rebuild. A site that cannot be edited, extended or moved is bought twice: once now, and again on the day it has to be replaced.

Which suggests a better question to ask of every quote you are holding, ours included. Not "what is the monthly fee" but "what happens if I stop paying it". Three specifics settle it: whose name is on the hosting account, whose name is on the domain and the Search Console property, and whether a different developer could pick up the codebase without starting again. A supplier who cannot answer all three cheerfully is quietly pricing a second build into the first one, and you will not see the invoice for it until it is unavoidable.

If search is part of the plan, budget for it separately rather than assuming the build absorbs it. UK small-business SEO retainers run **£150–£800 a month**, with most single-location local campaigns landing at **£500–£1,500**. Below roughly **£99 a month** you are in the tier that sends automated links and a monthly PDF with no strategy underneath. Our [SEO engagement](/services/seo) is scoped and priced on its own, and a site built properly does not require one in order to work.

## Who should not buy a £3,500 website from us

The honest disqualifications. A badly matched engagement is a worse outcome for us than no engagement at all.

- **You are still testing whether the idea works.** A subscription builder at £240–£360 a year answers a cheaper question first: does anyone want this? Buy the permanent site once demand is evidence rather than a hypothesis.
- **Your budget is under about £1,500.** A competent UK freelancer will build a good four- or five-page site for £1,200–£2,000. Spending £1,500 well beats spending £3,500 badly — and stretching to £3,500 usually means nothing is left for copy or photography, which is where the site would actually have improved.
- **You need to be live next week.** The shortest sensible timeline is measured in weeks, and what sets it is normally your copy, your photography and your internal approvals rather than the build.
- **WordPress is a requirement rather than a preference.** We do not work in it. That is a limit on us and not a verdict on the platform — but a WordPress site built reluctantly by the wrong hands is a worse asset than one built willingly by the right ones, and you should buy the second.
- **The website's whole job is to confirm you exist.** If work arrives by referral and the site only needs to carry a phone number and opening hours, the cheapest credible option is the correct one.

## Accessibility is an Equality Act question here, not a nice-to-have

WCAG 2.2 AA is the same technical standard in every country. What differs in the United Kingdom is the reason to buy it. Under the **Equality Act 2010**, service providers must make reasonable adjustments for disabled people, and an inaccessible website is a recognised risk area. That duty does not wait for someone to complain before it exists.

The reframing matters more than it sounds. Accessibility bought as a design preference is the first line cut when a budget tightens; accessibility bought as risk reduction is not, because the risk does not shrink when the budget does. On a build of ours the standard is verified automatically on every change and by a manual keyboard pass before launch — the only way a standard survives its second year. An audit signed off once begins decaying with the next content update.

The commercial half of the argument is quieter and larger. Keyboard-only navigation, screen-reader labelling, colour contrast that survives daylight on a phone, tap targets that work one-handed on a moving train — those are the conditions people genuinely browse in. Building for the hardest of them produces a site that is simply easier for everybody else.

## Two things that move a quote more than page count

The price card names the obvious levers. Two less obvious ones move a number further, and neither of them is really about the build.

**Content readiness.** The usual gating factor on a launch date is not code. It is copy that has not been written and photography that has not been taken. A fixed fee holds while the material exists and stops holding when a build waits weeks on a page of text only you can write. If you want the number on the card to be the number you pay, this is the part to start before you start us.

**Regulatory constraints on the architecture.** Some sites cost more to build correctly because the rules dictate the structure rather than the styling. UK clinics are the clearest case. Prescription-only medicines cannot be advertised to the public under the CAP Code, and where such a medicine may appear in a price list at all, that page must sit at least two clicks from the homepage, be reached through a consultation-first path, stay purely informational with no promotional content, and appear in neither the homepage nor the primary navigation. (Our reading of ASA/CAP guidance as at July 2026, not legal advice — and the kind of wording we would put through CAP's free Copy Advice service before a clinic site publishes.) That is an information-architecture specification with a cost attached, and it is not a styling decision that can be retrofitted after launch — see [websites for UK aesthetic clinics](/industries/aesthetic-clinics).

A redesign of a site that already ranks carries scope a new build does not, and it is the scope buyers are most tempted to cut. A card price assumes a site with nothing to lose. Years of accumulated search equity have to be moved across deliberately, and skipping that is not a saving — it is a decision to buy the same traffic a second time.

## "Web design" and "web development" are one purchase

UK buyers search for **web design**. The industry mostly sells **web development**. Two words, one job: the design decisions determine what gets built, and the build determines whether the design survives contact with a mid-range phone on a weak signal.

They are quoted here as a single fixed fee, because separating them is where the unpleasant surprises live — a design number approved first, and a build number that appears only once the design has gone too far to walk away from.

If the design genuinely is the whole job — an existing site that performs adequately but looks a decade old — that is [UI/UX design](/services/ui-ux-design), scoped and priced on its own.
