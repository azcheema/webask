## Read this before you commission an app

A brief that opens with "we need an app" usually ends, correctly, with a website. Better to
reach that conclusion on a page that costs nothing than three weeks into a paid discovery.

A mobile app is the most expensive thing on our [services](/services) list, and the only one
where the wrong answer bills you twice — once to build it, and again every year to keep it
alive. So this page argues both sides before it sells anything.

## When an app is genuinely the right buy

We take the project when at least two of these are true:

- **People use it repeatedly** — weekly, ideally daily. Habit is what an install buys, and a
  once-a-quarter interaction never earns space on someone's home screen.
- **It needs the phone itself.** Camera, background location, Bluetooth, barcode or document
  scanning, real offline use — the things a browser tab still does badly or not at all.
- **People are signed in.** Accounts, saved state, loyalty, order history, a balance:
  something that makes reopening the app better than opening it the first time.
- **Notifications are the mechanic, not the wish.** A delivery is out, a shift is available,
  a machine needs attention. Not "we'd like to push offers at them".
- **It is a tool for your own staff.** Field engineers, drivers, mobile therapists, stock
  counts. Internal apps are frequently the strongest case on this page, because usage is a
  condition of the job, not something the app has to win.

We say no — or scope something smaller — when:

- **The job is to be found.** If what you want is enquiries or bookings from search, you want
  a fast website and [SEO](/services/seo).
- **Customers touch you a few times a year.** A
  [dental practice](/industries/dental-practices) almost always does better with a
  booking-first website and automated recall than with an app patients open twice a year and
  then delete.
- **What you described is a logged-in area.** Portals, dashboards, booking back-ends and
  quoting tools usually belong in a [web app](/services/web-app-development) at £9,500, which
  runs everywhere and needs no store approval to change.
- **A competitor has one.** That tells you what a competitor's marketing budget decided, and
  nothing about whether their customers opened it twice.

## The second year is the real decision

For a website the market rule of thumb for ongoing cost is **15–20% of the build price per
year**, with a bare floor of £100–£300 a year for domain and hosting. Two things follow: the
cost scales with what you built, and under-spending for a year leaves a site that is dated
rather than broken.

Neither holds for an app. The work is set by Apple's and Google's release calendar rather
than by your feature list, so a deliberately simple app costs close to what a complicated one
costs to keep current. A [care plan](/services/maintenance-support) is a fixed line in the
budget from launch, not a percentage that shrinks when the scope does.

Which gives you a test to apply before you brief anybody, us included: **if the second-year
budget does not exist, the first year should not either.**

## Cross-platform, native, or no store at all

The framework question — React Native, Flutter, or native Swift and Kotlin — gets asked first
and matters least. It is settled in Discover, the reasoning goes into the written scope, and
it is one lever on the price among several.

The route is what actually costs money, and there are three, not two. Two put you in a store.
The third — no app at all, a mobile web experience built properly — gets skipped, and it is
right more often than the other two combined.

The case for it is commercial. The growth constraint on a typical UK small business is being
found — local search, the map pack, a page that loads on one bar of signal — and an app is
invisible to all of it. Nobody discovers a plumber, a clinic or a letting agent by browsing a
store. Which makes the store route a retention decision wearing an acquisition costume: worth
taking once there is a base of people to retain, and expensive before.

## UK data rules apply to the app, not just the website

There is no separate app regime to learn. **UK GDPR applies to the personal data your app
processes exactly as it applies to your website** — and with no cookie banner to hide behind,
the decisions become product decisions rather than a legal afterthought. Three get made during
design:

- **Which permissions you ask for, and when.** Location, contacts, camera, notifications.
  Asking for everything on first launch is the fastest way to lose the one you needed.
- **Whether anything tracks before consent.** Consent has to be freely given, specific,
  informed and unambiguous. The ICO wrote to 53 of the UK's top 100 websites over their cookie
  banners; the in-app equivalent gets built to that bar.
- **How much you collect at all.** Data minimisation is the cheapest compliance decision
  available, and it shrinks the store privacy declarations you have to stand behind.

Both stores make you declare what the app and its embedded SDKs do with data, and a
declaration that does not match the build is a rejection rather than a note. Which is why
those three belong in Design: the week you write the listing is too late to reopen the
question of background location.

Two UK specifics catch people out:

**Marketing to the list the app builds.** PECR governs marketing by email, and the rule turns
on the recipient's legal form: no prior consent for a limited company, LLP or public body, but
consent **is** required for a sole trader, an unincorporated partnership, or any freemail
address regardless of who uses it for work. UK small-business lists are full of the second
kind. Push is a different mechanism with its own consent model; we design the opt-in to the
stricter standard.

**The in-app review prompt.** Since 6 April 2025 the DMCC Act 2024 has made publishing fake
consumer reviews, or concealing incentivised ones, illegal in the UK — and the incentives that
count include cash, a discount, a freebie, an event invite or a financial interest. The CMA can
determine a breach and fine without recourse to the courts: for a business, up to 10% of global
annual turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher; for an individual, up to £150,000. So "rate
us five stars and get 10% off" is an enforcement risk, not a growth tactic. A prompt shown to
everyone, with nothing attached, is compliant and performs better anyway.

## If the app belongs to a regulated business

Aesthetic clinics, dental practices and beauty and wellness businesses are the three verticals
we specialise in, and the app medium creates regulatory surface a website does not.

**Store listings and push notifications are advertising.** The CAP Code reaches organic
content, not only paid ads, so an in-app treatment menu, the store listing and the wording of
a notification all sit under the same rules as the website — including the prohibition on
advertising prescription-only medicines to the public, which the ASA applies to implied
references and not only to brand names. The MHRA issued 47 enforcement notices to aesthetic
businesses in 2024, and the ASA now runs AI-powered proactive monitoring rather than waiting
for complaints.

**Booking flows carry eligibility rules, and one of them is already law.** England's licensing
scheme for non-surgical cosmetic procedures is **not yet in force as at July 2026**. The
Government's August 2025 consultation response proposed the shape it would take: a personal
licence for every individual performing a listed procedure rather than one business licence,
and a red / amber / green risk categorisation placing dermal fillers and the injectable
prescription-only treatments in amber, where a non-medical practitioner would work only under
the supervision of a named regulated healthcare professional. Proposed, not commenced — and a
booking screen that states it as current law is wrong today.

What is already in force is narrower and firmer: since **1 October 2021** it has been a criminal
offence in England to administer these treatments for a cosmetic purpose to under-18s. That one
belongs in the eligibility check now. The rest belongs in a build you can switch on when the
scheme commences, which is the practical reason to keep eligibility rules in configuration
rather than hard-coded into a screen.

For dental practices, the GDC's requirement for genuine, un-incentivised reviews sits on top of
the DMCC rules above. None of this is legal advice — we are not solicitors — but it is the
standard we build to, and it is why a clinic app is scoped with the same copy review as a
clinic website.

## What working with us actually looks like

Every engagement runs the same four stages — [our process](/process) sets out the artefacts and
what we need at each. Two things are specific to mobile.

**Approval happens on a phone.** The only honest review of an app is you holding a build in the
situation it is meant for — in the van, at the counter, between patients, on a platform with no
signal. So sign-off is weeks of ordinary use, not a demo in the final sprint.

**There is no account-manager layer.** Whoever scopes the app writes the code and answers when
a store review comes back with a question. WebAsk is fully remote with no UK office — which for
app work matters less than anywhere else, since a build installs on a phone in Manchester or
Cardiff the same minute it installs on ours.

## Two honest next steps

If you already know what people would do on the app in a normal week, ask for a scope. A
30-minute call booked through [contact](/contact) is enough to test whether the idea survives a
build estimate, and within three working days you get a written scope or, where we are plainly
the wrong firm, a referral.

If you are still deciding, the app is the wrong question to open on. Start with the
[free audit](/free-audit) of the site you already have. If the finding is that your mobile
website is what costs you enquiries, that is a cheaper problem — and the one to fix first.
