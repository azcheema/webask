## An online shop is a conversion problem and a consumer-law surface

Every other page on a business website has one job: persuade. A shop has two. It has to move a visitor from a listing page to a confirmed order, and it has to do that inside UK rules that bite on the shop's _features_ — reviews, price display, basket-recovery email, tracking tags — rather than on its adjectives.

A shop built with only the first job in mind has a recognisable shape. The reviews widget came with the theme, the basket sequence came with the email app, the cookie banner came from a free plugin, and the advertising pixel fires regardless of what the visitor clicked. That is what happens when a build stops at "it looks good and the checkout works" — and since April 2025 the second job has had teeth the regulator can use without going near a courtroom.

## Reviews are the most exposed feature on a UK shop

Since **6 April 2025** the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 has made it illegal in the UK to write, commission or publish fake consumer reviews, or to conceal that a review was incentivised. "Incentivised" is drawn broadly: cash, a discount, a freebie, an event invite, a financial interest. Traders must also not present reviews in a misleading way, and **aggregate scores are explicitly in scope**.

The enforcement design is the part people miss. The **CMA can determine a breach and fine directly, without recourse to the courts** — up to **10% of global annual turnover or £300,000**, whichever is higher, and up to **£150,000** for an individual. Its opening posture was supportive, but in **March 2026 it opened investigations into five named businesses** over review handling.

Set that against the failure modes a storefront build has to guard against: demo reviews arriving with the theme and surviving launch, a review app offers a discount for a photo review and discloses it nowhere, and `AggregateRating` fires on every product whether or not a real review exists.

So the review system is a named part of the build:

- **Nothing seeded, imported or written by us.** A new shop launches with an empty review component and an honest "no reviews yet" state.
- **Verified-purchase provenance stored against every review.** The question a regulator asks is not "is this review flattering" — it is "can you show it is real".
- **Incentive disclosure built into the component**, shown on the review itself, not buried in a policy page nobody opens.
- **`Product`, `Review` and `AggregateRating` markup that only fires where real reviews exist**, with the marked-up aggregate matching what the page displays.
- **Moderation that does not quietly bury the negatives** — publishing only the four- and five-star reviews is a misleading presentation, and the easiest breach to prove from outside.

## Basket recovery email, and the rule that decides whether you may send it

Abandoned-basket and win-back email is the highest-return automation in ecommerce, and in the UK it stands on a narrow footing: the **PECR soft opt-in**. That applies only where all three of these hold.

1. The details were obtained **during a sale, or during negotiations for a sale** — which is precisely what a started checkout is.
2. You are marketing **your own similar goods or services**.
3. A **clear opt-out was offered when the details were collected, and appears in every message you send**.

Two consequences change the build. **Any freemail address is treated as an individual**, whichever business the person uses it for — so a `gmail.com` customer gets the individual regime whatever your segmentation thinks. And **bought or scraped lists never qualify**: a list inherited with an acquisition is not automatically mailable.

Consent therefore belongs in the build, not in the email tool afterwards. The capture carries its opt-out and a purpose statement at the point of collection, and every record stores **which basis applies** — soft opt-in from a started checkout, or explicit consent from a signup — because a subscriber who never began a purchase isn't covered at all. Unsubscribes write back to the shop, not only to the email platform, and nothing is pre-ticked. Where the automation is the larger part of the job it belongs in [CRM automation](/services/crm-automation) rather than here.

## Price display, VAT, and the screen where a basket dies

Pricing transparency is the CMA's territory, and of the two things worth saying here only one is a rule. The rule: **prices shown to UK consumers are VAT-inclusive, or the exclusion is made unmistakably clear.** A shop showing an ex-VAT figure to a consumer because its theme was built for a trade catalogue has a real problem, and the fix is a template decision made once rather than a plugin bought at month six.

The other is only a preference: a delivery charge that first appears at the payment step is the most reliable way to lose a basket you had already won, so the templates surface a figure or an honest estimate as early as the shop can calculate one. Worth knowing which of the two you are being told.

## Tags, pixels, and the cookie banner that does nothing

A shop runs more third-party code than any other kind of site — analytics, advertising pixels, review apps, chat, heatmaps. Under PECR, **"Accept all" and "Reject all" need equal visual prominence**, "by continuing to browse you agree" is not consent, and nothing non-essential may be set **before** consent. The ICO wrote to **53 of the UK's top 100 websites** about exactly this, and PECR exposure runs to **£17.5m or 4% of global turnover**.

The commonest failure is the banner itself — rejection made harder than acceptance, which is the ICO's stated main enforcement focus. The second, which survives a banner rebuild, is an honest banner sitting above a theme that hard-codes the pixel into the layout, so it fires either way: invisible in a code review, obvious in the network tab. Which is why the check belongs in the network tab and not in a compliance checklist.

## The floor a shop gets built to

Two sets of numbers sit underneath the build, and neither is a result being claimed on your behalf — they are the budgets this site is written against. **Speed:** Cumulative Layout Shift at or under 0.05 and main-thread blocking under 200ms are hard gates on every commit, so a change that regresses them fails before it merges; Largest Contentful Paint is written against 2.0 seconds and carried as a warning, because a throttled build server reports a pessimistically slow number and the field data is what settles it; Interaction to Next Paint cannot be measured in a lab at all, so it is watched in real visitor data after launch. **Accessibility:** WCAG 2.2 AA, an automated score of 100 that either passes or fails the build, a manual keyboard pass.

Where you measure matters as much as the number. A homepage hero can be tuned until it reports beautifully while the listing and product templates — the two that actually take money — are never sampled. Google holds roughly **92.7% of UK search**, so treating Core Web Vitals as optional is an undiversified bet. The accessibility floor is the Equality Act 2010 in UK terms, and on a shop it bites hardest at a checkout that cannot be completed with a keyboard.

## Shopify, WooCommerce or headless — and who carries the compliance surface

Platform choice is usually framed as control versus overhead. In the UK that is incomplete: the platform also decides how much of the consumer-law surface you personally own, and how much gets settled by an app someone installed in ten minutes.

| Approach                      | The case for it                                                          | What the compliance surface looks like                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Shopify, seriously themed** | Payments, fraud tooling and patching stop being your problem             | Narrowed, not removed. Reviews, consent and tax display follow the apps you add, so app choice is a legal decision |
| **WooCommerce on WordPress**  | An existing WordPress estate, unusual product logic, or owning the lot   | All yours — consent, reviews, VAT display, updates. Fine if someone owns it; quiet drift if nobody does            |
| **Headless**                  | The storefront experience is the product, or speed is a competitive edge | The strongest position on consent: nothing fires unless you wrote the code that allows it                          |

No platform absolves the trader. When the CMA writes about a review, or the ICO about a tag, it writes to the shop rather than to the vendor whose widget produced it — which argues for the smallest platform that can genuinely carry your catalogue. Every extra layer is one more place a rule gets implemented by accident.

## Moving off WooCommerce, Wix or a marketplace — and what not to bring

A migration is usually described as a URL problem. Since April 2025 it is a provenance problem too, and that is the half nobody plans for. Reviews that cannot be traced to a real customer are the one asset to leave behind deliberately: republishing them on a new shop is a fresh act of publication, so an unevidenced corpus stops being inherited social proof and becomes inherited liability. Have that conversation before the build, not during cutover week.

The search side is ordinary discipline, and two numbers are worth knowing in advance. Expect a **two-to-four-week ranking wobble** after cutover before things settle, and keep the redirects for **at least twelve months** — holding them longer costs nothing, and dropping them early is the one migration decision that cannot be walked back.

## If you already sell online, the order matters

The temptation when fixing an existing shop is to work easiest-first rather than most-exposed-first. Ordered by exposure instead: reviews, as the one carrying direct fines with no court in the way. Then consent, because a pixel firing before the banner is trivial to verify from outside the business. Then price and delivery display, usually a template fix rather than a project. Speed last — it pays commercially and has no regulator attached, which is exactly why it tends to get done first.
