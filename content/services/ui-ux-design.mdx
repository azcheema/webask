Most UX pages sell taste. This one starts somewhere less comfortable: in the UK, a handful of
the decisions made in a design file are the ones a regulator eventually looks at.

Not the typeface. The relative prominence of two buttons. The contrast of a link against the
surface behind it. Whether a page sits one click from the homepage or two. Those get decided in
the first fortnight, and by the time anyone checks them the site has been live a year and the
fix is a rebuild.

The conversion work is still the job — clearer structure, less friction, fewer people
abandoning halfway through an enquiry. What is different here is that a few of those
decisions are load-bearing, and get designed as if they are.

## The consent banner is a design problem, not a cookie plugin

Under PECR, consent has to meet the UK GDPR Article 4(11) standard: freely given, specific,
informed and unambiguous. Most UK sites treat that as something a plugin takes care of. A
plugin ships a component; the standard is met or missed in how that component is designed.

The failure is prominence. **"Accept all" and "Reject all" need genuinely equal visual
prominence.** A banner where accepting is a solid brand-coloured button and rejecting is grey
text below it fails the freely-given test. That is the most common failure on UK sites and the
ICO's main enforcement focus — it wrote to **53 of the UK's top 100 websites** about cookie
banners, and PECR exposure runs to **£17.5m or 4% of global turnover**.

Equal prominence is not a legal question answered at the end of a build. It is contrast ratio,
type scale, button weight, reading order and tap-target size — decided in Figma, in about ten
minutes, usually by someone who has never been told it matters.

What we design to, and hand over documented:

- Two actions at the same visual weight — same size, same hierarchy, same hit area. If the
  reject path is one tap harder, it is not equal.
- Nothing non-essential fires before a choice is made — not "on load, we will ask in a
  moment". No pre-ticked boxes, no "by continuing to browse you agree", no cookie wall
  withholding the content until acceptance. None of the three is consent.
- A designed way back in: a persistent cookie settings link in the footer, in the layout from
  the start, because consent has to be re-obtainable.
- A cookie policy that enumerates each cookie, its purpose, duration and provider — a
  content-design job with a real table in it, not a boilerplate paste.

## Accessibility is an Equality Act question before it is a Lighthouse score

We build to WCAG 2.2 AA. In the UK the framing that moves a budget is the **Equality Act
2010**: service providers must make reasonable adjustments, and an inaccessible website is a
recognised risk area. Risk reduction plus reach — not a nice-to-have, and not a badge.

Nearly all of it is settled in design rather than in code. Colour contrast. Visible focus
states, the first thing removed when someone finds them ugly. Target size. The order in which
things take keyboard focus, which is the order they were drawn in. An engineer can implement an
inaccessible design perfectly, and frequently does.

So contrast gets measured when a colour is chosen, not assumed. The teal on this site
clears white text at 5.47:1 — a number that arrived by measurement and came in lower than the
estimate it replaced, which is the entire argument for measuring. A brand colour that fails is
cheap to change in week one and expensive to unpick once it is in forty components.

Underneath that sits a gate rather than a promise: our pipeline fails a build that scores below
100 for accessibility in Lighthouse CI, with axe-core and a manual keyboard pass alongside it.
That is a constraint we work under, not a result we are inviting you to admire.

None of it survives on its own. Equal prominence agreed in a review and then rebuilt by
eye stops being equal prominence; a contrast ratio that passes in a mockup and fails in the
component library has passed nothing. So the rules that carry regulatory weight get written into
the component definition — focus, error and disabled states, target sizes — where whoever adds a
screen next year inherits them without having to be told they exist.

## Information architecture as a compliance deliverable

This is the argument nobody in UK UX seems to make, and it is the one that matters most if you
run a clinic.

CAP Code rule 12.12 prohibits advertising a prescription-only medicine to the public. Most
practitioners half-know that. What almost nobody explains is the conditional permission
underneath it: a price list covering such a treatment **may** exist — provided it sits at
least two clicks from the homepage, is reached through a consultation-first path, stays
purely informational with no promotional framing, and appears in neither the homepage nor the
primary navigation. (Our reading of ASA/CAP guidance as at July 2026, not legal advice.)

Read that as a specification rather than a warning, because that is what it is. Navigation
depth. A required linking path. An exclusion from the top-level menu. A sitemap that agrees
with the menu. Every one is an information-architecture deliverable — what a UX engagement
produces, and what a copywriting pass cannot give you. No phrasing rescues a menu that lists
the wrong thing at the top level, and the softer substitutes clinics reach for instead of the
brand name have themselves been ruled against as implied promotion — Venus Beauty Lounge in
2015, Beauty Boutique Aesthetics in 2019 and Glowery Ltd in 2023 among the upheld rulings — so
rewording is not the escape hatch either.

We call the pattern **consultation-first architecture**: concern-led entry pages, the detailed
material reached through consultation content, and the navigation, internal links and sitemap
all built to the same rule instead of contradicting one another. More on how it is built on
[websites for UK aesthetic clinics](/industries/aesthetic-clinics).

Dentistry has its own version: the CMA opened a market study into the **£8.4bn private
dentistry sector in March 2026**, pricing transparency squarely in scope — and a price list is
an interface long before it is a policy. See
[websites for UK dental practices](/industries/dental-practices).

If nobody regulates your trade, none of this binds you. The discipline still transfers, because
it is the discipline a silo runs on: every page with one obvious parent, one reason to exist,
and links that point up to it, so relevance concentrates instead of scattering across a menu
that grew by accretion.

## The testimonial slider is a regulated component now

Since **6 April 2025** the DMCC Act 2024 has made publishing fake consumer reviews illegal in
the UK, along with concealing incentivised ones — and traders must not present reviews in a
misleading way, aggregate scores included. The CMA can determine a breach and fine directly,
without a court: up to **10% of global annual turnover or £300,000**, whichever is higher. In
**March 2026** it opened investigations into five named businesses over review handling.

That turns ordinary interface choices into decisions with consequences. A five-star average
needs its sample size beside it. An incentive needs a disclosure sitting with the review, not a
note on another page. A slider that quietly shows only the flattering ones is a presentation
problem with a statutory definition. On a clinic site the same component answers to a second
rule as well — CAP 12.18 bars celebrity and health-professional endorsement of a
prescription-only medicine — so what a testimonial is allowed to display there is narrower than
the honesty test alone would suggest.

The clearest example we can point at is this domain. The old site it replaces carried
testimonials attributed to people who do not exist, next to a staff page listing colleagues who
were never hired, for a one-person business. That was ours, and it is coming down as part of
this launch — in that order, because the new site has to be answering on the domain before the
old one goes, or every indexed URL dies into a 404 in between.

This site carries no testimonials at all. WebAsk is new in the UK, and there is nobody yet who
could give one honestly. That makes a weaker page than a carousel of five-star quotes would. It
is also the only version of it anyone making the argument above can honestly publish.

A freelance build sits around **£800–£3,000**, with a typical four-to-five-page site at

The starting figure on the price card above earns its keep in one situation: when the interface
itself is where the risk sits, or when the structure is what is broken. A nicer template fixes
neither.

Three things this is not:

  most of what this page describes: banner prominence, navigation depth and focus order all
  _are_ structure. You would be paying for rendering, and rendering is cheaper elsewhere.

open up local search. It would also be precisely the class of claim this page argues against.

## The question worth asking before you scope anything

Which single screen would a regulator look at first? On most UK sites it is the consent banner.
On a clinic site it is the navigation. On any site old enough to have a slider, it is the
reviews. If the answer is obvious to you, your brief is nearly written. If it is not, that is
the finding worth having, and it is what the [free audit](/free-audit) goes looking for.
