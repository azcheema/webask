A one-star review lands on your Google profile. It looks fake, or it says something about your business that you know is untrue. So how do you contact Google to take down a review?

You report it, and Google decides. Its help pages describe a Report option next to each review, and a Reviews Management Tool to track the report. In Google's words, "only those that violate Google policies are eligible for removal". For content you believe breaks the law, Google has a separate legal route.

Checked against Google's help pages and policy, the legislation and the CMA's guidance on 3 October 2026. WebAsk is a web agency, not a law firm. This summarises published policy and law; it is not legal advice.

<Callout variant="note" title="TL;DR">
  Report a review that breaks Google's content policy, and use Google's separate legal request only
  for content you believe is unlawful. Nobody outside Google decides what its policy removes. Ask a
  solicitor about the law, and reply calmly to any review that stays up.
</Callout>

## How do I contact Google to take down a review?

Google's page on how to [report inappropriate reviews](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4596773?hl=en-GB) sets out five steps:

1. "Go to your Business Profile."
2. "Select Read reviews."
3. "Next to the review that you'd like to flag, select Report."
4. "Select the reason to flag the review." Google's examples are "Spam" and "Profanity".
5. "Select Send report."

You can also report from the [Reviews Management Tool](https://support.google.com/business/workflow/9945796?hl=en-GB), a separate page you sign in to. Google says: "Use this tool to report review removals and check review status."

Before you report, read Google's warning on the same page.

> "Do not report a review just because you disagree with it or dislike it. Google doesn't get involved in conflict between businesses and customers."

Google also lets you report a reviewer's profile in the Google Maps app. Its [profile page](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/9968060?hl=en-GB) says: "Only report user profiles that contribute content that violates Google policies."

Two more routes are covered below: a form for review extortion, and a legal route for content you believe is unlawful.

### If someone demands money to remove reviews

Google has a separate [extortion page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16404809?hl=en-GB). This is the scam it describes:

> "These scams may involve a sudden increase in 1-star and 2-star reviews on your Google Business Profile, followed by someone demanding money, goods or services in exchange for removing the negative reviews."

Its advice is plain.

> "Do not engage with or pay the malicious individuals. This can encourage further attempts and doesn't guarantee the removal of reviews."

Use Google's "merchant extortion report form" instead. Google asks for screenshots that show "the date, time and the sender's contact information, to the extent possible", links to the reviews, and when you first saw the reviews and the demand. It says its team "thoroughly investigates the claim and notifies you about the results of the investigation". The form is "exclusively" for a direct extortion attempt.

## What Google's policy removes, and what stays up

The policy behind review removal is Google's [content policy for Maps](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en-GB). It is Google's rule for its own platform, not the law. It covers "Text reviews" and "Ratings", and it applies "wherever you are in the world". Its headings include fake engagement, rating manipulation, misrepresentation, harassment, offensive content, personal information and off-topic content.

What it allows matters as much:

> "We do allow content that describes negative experiences in a respectful manner."

Our reading: _negative_, _unfair_ and _exaggerated_ are not headings on Google's list. Google's tips add: "Negative reviews aren't necessarily a sign of poor business practices."

Clinics should note two lines. Google allows "An individual's name if they are a public-facing professional conducting business under their name", and its examples include "Doctors". Its [privacy page](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7401426?hl=en-GB) does not allow "private or confidential information" such as "medical records".

## After you report: the statuses, the appeal and the wait

Google gives one timescale for a review report: "Review evaluation typically takes several days." You can follow it in the Reviews Management Tool. These are its statuses, in Google's words:

| Status                                   | What Google says it means                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Decision pending                         | "The review is flagged, but it hasn't been evaluated yet."                        |
| Report reviewed – no policy violation    | "The review was evaluated and no policy violation was found."                     |
| Escalated – check your email for updates | "The appeal has been escalated and you'll get an email about the final decision." |

If Google finds no violation, its page says: "If a flagged review doesn't qualify for removal, submit a one-time appeal." In the tool, choose "Appeal eligible reviews". "You can select up to 10 reviews." Then fill in Google's form. Google emails the result. Its page says that if the review breaks its policies, "it will be removed". If not, "it will remain live", and its status will show as "Escalated – check your email for updates". So "Escalated" does not mean a review is on its way down. Google's page gives no time for an appeal.

Keep a note of each review you report: the date, the reason you chose and each status. The appeal is one-time, so that note is your record.

You can also report a review in Google Maps, and the app keeps its own [Reports section](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7445749?hl=en-GB). Its labels differ, such as "Processing report" and "Content not removed". If Google does not remove the content, it says "you may be eligible to submit one appeal".

## Google's separate route for content you believe is unlawful

If you believe a review breaks the law, Google has a separate route. Google's Maps help says it "may remove content" for "non-policy violating reasons", including "The content violates applicable law." Its [Legal removals](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/16426540?hl=en-GB) page says "users can report content that they believe is in violation of local law".

That report starts at Google's [Report content on Google](https://support.google.com/legal/troubleshooter/1114905?hl=en-GB) page. Choose "Google Maps and related products", then local listings and reviews, then "Legal reasons to report content". One option reads:

> "Defamation: Report content that defames you or your business/organisation (typically a false statement damaging reputation)"

Others include "Personal data / privacy" and "Court order". The Defamation option ends: "Click Create request to submit a request to our team."

Two lines on the same Google page matter before you file.

- "You may report the same content through both legal and content/product policy reporting paths, but you must file each report separately." A policy report "does not serve as legal notice".
- "Typically, we remove or restrict access to the content only in the country/region where it is deemed to be illegal." For policy breaches, Google says it "typically" removes or restricts access "globally".

None of the Google pages read for this post gives a timescale for a legal request.

## What UK defamation law says, nation by nation

A review that says something untrue about your business may raise a question of defamation. That is a matter for a solicitor, and the law differs across the UK. This is what the statutes say on legislation.gov.uk, as read on 3 October 2026.

| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| England and Wales | [Defamation Act 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/section/1), which "extends to England and Wales only" ([s.17(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/section/17)) | "A statement is not defamatory unless its publication has caused or is likely to cause serious harm to the reputation of the claimant." For "a body that trades for profit", harm is not "serious harm" unless it "has caused or is likely to cause the body serious financial loss" (s.1)                     | [Section 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/section/5) is a defence for "the operator of a website" who shows "it was not the operator who posted the statement". It is defeated if the claimant shows they could not identify the poster, gave the operator "a notice of complaint", and the operator "failed to respond" as the [regulations](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3028/made) require                                                                                                          | After judgment for the claimant, the court "may order" the operator "to remove the statement" ([s.13](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/section/13))                                                                       |

Someone whose only role is "operating or providing any equipment, system or service by means of which the statement is retrieved, copied, distributed or made available in electronic form" is not one of them                                                                                     | In defamation proceedings, a court "may order" a website operator "to remove the statement", or to include "a prominent notice" that it is subject to the proceedings ([s.30](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2021/10/section/30)) |
| Northern Ireland  | [Defamation Act (Northern Ireland) 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nia/2022/30/contents), in operation since 7 June 2022                                                                 | The 2022 Act's list of sections has no serious-harm test. [Section 1 of the Defamation Act 1996](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/31/section/1), which extends to Northern Ireland and to England and Wales ([s.18](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/31/section/18)), is a defence for a person who shows they were not the "author, editor or publisher", "took reasonable care", and "did not know, and had no reason to believe" that what they did "caused or contributed to the publication of a defamatory statement" | The 2022 Act "does not affect any power of a court that exists apart from this Act" ([s.9](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nia/2022/30/section/9))                                                                                     |

Two points about England and Wales. Our reading: section 5 and its regulations are a defence for the operator, not a right to have a review taken down. The regulations' schedule is headed "Actions to be taken by operator in response to notice of complaint in order to maintain section 5(2) defence". And under [section 10](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/section/10), a court cannot hear a defamation claim against someone who was not the author, editor or publisher. The exception is where the court is satisfied that it is "not reasonably practicable" to bring the claim against the author, editor or publisher.

None of the sources read for this post says whether Google is an "operator", or an "author, editor or publisher", under these Acts. Speak to a solicitor before you act on any of this.

One caution applies to genuine reviews. The Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) has published [guidance on fake reviews](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67eeb64fe9c76fa33048c790/CMA208_-_Fake_reviews_guidance.pdf), known as CMA208. At paragraph 4.4, it says "suppressing genuine negative reviews is problematic". Traders "should not" interfere with "the ability and willingness of reviewers to leave negative reviews in the first place", for example "through threats of harm or legal action".

## Why nobody outside Google can promise removal

On its reporting page, Google says only reviews that break its policies are "eligible for removal". Its Maps help adds: "We reserve the right to either reinstate or take down content, irrespective of whether your report was accepted or not accepted." Paying someone who threatens your reviews "doesn't guarantee the removal of reviews", Google says. The reviewer can edit or delete their own review. And a court "may order" a website operator to remove the statement: in England and Wales after judgment for the claimant, and in Scotland in defamation proceedings.

Nobody outside Google decides what Google's policy removes. WebAsk does not sell review removal.

## Replying to a review that stays up

A reply is public. Google's [reply page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474050?hl=en-GB) says it is posted "under the customer's review", and "your personal name won't be shown". Google checks each reply to make sure it will "follow Google's content policies". It adds:

> "The person who wrote the review is notified when you reply. Customers can still change their review after reading your reply."

Google's [ranking tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en-GB) say: "When you reply to customer reviews, it shows that you value their feedback." Its [reply tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en-GB) include:

- "Keep it short and simple".
- "Never share the reviewer's private info. Do not attack them personally."
- "You can request the reviewer to contact you, in person, email or phone to help resolve the issue."
- "Check your records about the reviewer and their visit."
- "Admit mistakes that were made, but don't take responsibility for things outside your control."

Offer nothing for a changed or deleted review. Our guide to [what UK law allows when you ask for Google reviews](/blog/google-reviews-dmcc-act-uk) explains why. For dental practices, our [GDC website guide](/blog/gdc-compliant-dental-website) sets out how we would write a reply to a patient's review.

## A checklist for each review

1. Does it break Google's policy, or is it only negative? Through Google's report, only reviews that break its policies are "eligible for removal".
2. Which reason in Google's form fits? Its examples are "Spam" and "Profanity".
3. Does the reviewer's profile contribute content that breaks Google's policies? Report the profile in the Google Maps app as well.
4. Is someone demanding money to remove reviews? Use Google's extortion form, and do not pay.
5. Do you believe it breaks the law? That is a separate legal report, and a question for a solicitor.
6. Have you noted the date, the reason and each status? The appeal is one-time.
7. Is your reply short and calm, with nothing private in it and nothing offered for a change?
