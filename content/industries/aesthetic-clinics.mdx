Most UK aesthetic clinics are sold a website by someone who has never read the CAP Code. It
looks expensive, loads slowly on a phone, and — on nearly every site we audit — breaches the
advertising rules on the first treatment page.

That last part is not a design opinion. It is the reason this page exists.

## The rule almost every clinic site breaks

A prescription-only medicine cannot be advertised to the public in the United Kingdom. Not on
a treatment page, not in a price list, not in an FAQ, not in a photo caption, and not in a
social post linking back to any of them. The prohibition is blanket, the ASA enforces it, and
rulings are published under the clinic's own name.

Two things make this harder than it first sounds.

**Euphemisms count.** Swapping the brand name for a softer phrase does not solve the problem —
the ASA has repeatedly treated wrinkle-relaxing wording as implied promotion of the same
medicine, and upheld complaints on exactly that basis. A great many clinics have made precisely
this substitution and believe they are now compliant.

**What is permitted is the consultation, not the treatment.** You may advertise a consultation
for the concern a patient wants addressed. That is a genuinely workable commercial position —
it is simply a different website to the one most clinics currently have.

## This is an architecture problem, not a copywriting one

Here is the part almost nobody explains properly, and the reason a rewrite alone will not fix
your site.

A price list covering a prescription-only treatment **may** exist. The conditions are
structural: it needs to sit at least two clicks from the homepage, be reached through
consultation-led pages, stay purely informational with no promotional framing, and appear in
neither the homepage nor the primary navigation.

Read that again as a specification rather than a warning, because that is what it is. It
describes a navigation hierarchy, an internal linking pattern, a sitemap and a set of page
templates. It is a build brief.

Which is why changing a few words on your existing site does not get you there. If your
treatment pages sit in the main menu — and on most clinic sites they do — the structure itself
is the problem, and no amount of careful phrasing fixes a menu.

## Consultation-first architecture

This is the pattern we build, and it is what lets a compliant clinic site still perform:

- **Concern-led entry pages.** Visitors arrive searching for an outcome, not a product name.
  Pages are organised around the concern and the consultation — which is both what the rules
  permit and, conveniently, closer to how people actually search.
- **A consultation-first path.** The route to any detailed treatment information runs through
  consultation content, satisfying the structural condition rather than working around it.
- **Clean primary navigation.** Nothing that must stay out of the main menu appears in it, and
  the sitemap and internal links agree — a compliant menu with a contradicting XML sitemap is
  not compliant.
- **Enquiry capture that survives the restructure.** Booking, intake, reminders and follow-up
  run through your CRM, so a more careful site does not become a quieter one.

## The 2026 licensing scheme, stated accurately

As at **July 2026** the England licensing scheme for non-surgical cosmetic procedures is **not
yet in force**. The Government published its consultation response in **August 2025** — 11,848
responses — proposing a **red, amber and green** risk categorisation, and signalled a further
consultation on the highest-risk procedures in **spring 2026**. Since **October 2021** it has
been a criminal offence in England to administer botulinum toxin or filler for a cosmetic
purpose to anyone under 18.
