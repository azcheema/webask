Most of what a salon, spa or wellness studio sells is not a medicine. If you infuse or inject
anything, though, read the third rule below first — on the advertising question you may be
closer to an aesthetic clinic than to a salon.

For everything else, the rule that decides what you may say is a different one, and it turns
on **what you claim the treatment does.**

## The claim problem

Describing a treatment — what it is, how it works, what it feels like, who it suits — is safe
ground. Asserting what it _achieves_ is where sites get into trouble.

Any objective claim needs evidence behind it. Past that, which rule applies turns on what the
thing **is** — not on what you call your business — and there are three.

- **Treatments, spaces and experiences.** Substantiation. Hold evidence for the claim you make.
- **Food and supplements.** Only claims **authorised on the GB nutrition and health claims
  register** — in force since **January 2021** — or claims that mean the same thing to a
  consumer, may be used. That is CAP Code section 15. "Boosts your immune system" is not a
  description; it is a health claim, and it has to be one the register carries.
- **Anything injected or infused.** Medicines law, which is stricter than either. A medicinal
  claim is not permitted unless that specific product is licensed as a medicine for that
  purpose, and advertising an unauthorised medicinal product in Great Britain is a **criminal
  offence** under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012.

The third is the one that surprises people, because it is where an IV drip or a vitamin
injection sits. The ASA has upheld complaints against IV clinics on exactly this point, over
claims that a drip could prevent or treat COVID-19 when the product was not licensed to do it
— and in those cases the MHRA's position was that any mention of that condition brought the
product inside medicines regulation. On the advertising question an IV bar has more in common
with an aesthetic clinic than with a salon.

This matters commercially rather than just legally, because the claim may be doing less work
than it appears to. People book a vitamin drip, a facial or a recovery session because of how
they expect to feel and how the place looks. A service page can sell the experience without
carrying the claim that creates the exposure.

## Reviews: the rule that changed in 2025

The DMCC Act 2024 applies here exactly as it does anywhere else. Since **April 2025**,
commissioning or publishing fake reviews is illegal, and the CMA can determine a breach and
fine directly — up to **10% of global annual turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher**.

The specific limb that bites here is incentives, and the detail is worth having exactly right:
an incentive includes cash, a discount, a freebie, an event invite or a financial interest, and
what the Act prohibits is **concealing** it. Prize draws and discount-for-review offers are
common in salon marketing, and it is the undisclosed ones that carry the exposure — the offer
itself is not the breach. A review flow that asks every client at the right moment, with
nothing attached, sidesteps the question entirely, and because it samples everyone rather than
the people a discount motivated it is a more accurate reflection of the business.

## When the economics are retention, the site should be too

This is the real difference between building for a wellness business and building for most
other sectors. Where the money is in the second visit, the package, the membership and the
rebooking, the site has a different job to do.

A brochure with a booking button attached is not built to carry memberships, packages or
rebooking; a site built around them is, however good the brochure looks. What we build instead:

- **Booking as the primary action, everywhere.** Online booking that works in seconds on a
  phone, at eleven at night, without a phone call.
- **Memberships, packages and gift cards as first-class features.** Recurring billing, prepaid
  series, balances and renewals tracked automatically — not a note in a diary.
- **Retention automation.** Follow-up after a first visit, a prompt to rebook before the client
  drifts, win-back for lapsed clients, and a review request that goes to every client, timed to
  the point in the visit cycle where asking makes sense.
- **Missed-contact recovery.** The leaks here are calls during treatments and messages after
  hours. A voice agent answers and books; messaging automation captures and qualifies the
  late-night traffic that would otherwise go cold.

## Fast, on a phone, with the images intact

Your site will be judged on a phone, and your brand is visual — which is an awkward
combination, because images are what make sites slow.

Total page weight, layout shift and blocking time are budgets a build has to pass before it
ships, so a gallery cannot quietly get heavier release by release. Load time itself we write
against a two-second target and then watch in real visitor data, which is the only place it can
be judged honestly. Your space and your work should look as good on a small screen as they do
in person, and still load before someone gives up. That is an engineering problem with a known
solution, not a trade-off you have to accept.
