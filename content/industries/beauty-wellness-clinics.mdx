Most of what a salon, spa or wellness studio sells is not a medical treatment, which means the
advertising rules that dominate aesthetics and dentistry mostly do not apply to you.

A different one does, and it catches this sector constantly: **what you claim the treatment
does.**

## The claim problem

Describing a treatment — what it is, how it works, what it feels like, who it suits — is safe
ground and generally under-used. Asserting what it _achieves_ is where sites get into trouble.

Any health or wellbeing benefit you state has to be substantiable. Claims about nutrition and
health specifically — the cluster that forms around IV and vitamin therapy, immunity, energy,
detoxification and recovery — are tightly controlled, and only authorised claims may be made.
"Boosts your immune system" is not a description; it is a health claim, and it needs to be one
you are permitted to make.

This matters commercially rather than just legally, because the claim is usually doing less
work than the operator thinks. People book a vitamin drip, a facial or a recovery session
because of how they expect to feel and how the place looks, not because of a physiological
assertion in the second paragraph. Service pages that sell the experience convert perfectly
well without the claim that creates the exposure.

## Reviews: the rule that changed in 2025

The DMCC Act 2024 applies here exactly as it does anywhere else. Since **April 2025**,
commissioning or publishing fake reviews is illegal, and the CMA can determine a breach and
fine directly — up to **10% of global turnover or £300,000**.

The specific risk in this sector is incentives, because prize draws and discount-for-review
offers are so normal in salon marketing that they rarely register as a compliance question at
all. A review flow that asks every client at the right moment, with nothing attached, is both
safer and — because it samples everyone rather than the people motivated by a discount — a
more accurate reflection of the business.

## Your economics are retention, so the site should be too

This is the real difference between building for a wellness business and building for most
other sectors. The money is not in acquisition. It is in the second visit, the package, the
membership and the rebooking.

A site built as a brochure with a booking button attached will underperform one built around
that reality, however good it looks. What we build instead:

- **Booking as the primary action, everywhere.** Online booking that works in seconds on a
  phone, at eleven at night, without a phone call.
- **Memberships, packages and gift cards as first-class features.** Recurring billing, prepaid
  series, balances and renewals tracked automatically — not a note in a diary.
- **Retention automation.** Follow-up after a first visit, a prompt to rebook before the client
  drifts, win-back for lapsed clients, and a review request timed to when they are happiest.
- **Missed-contact recovery.** The leaks here are calls during treatments and messages after
  hours. A voice agent answers and books; messaging automation captures and qualifies the
  late-night traffic that would otherwise go cold.

## Fast, on a phone, with the images intact

Nearly all of your traffic is mobile, and your brand is visual — which is an awkward
combination, because images are what make sites slow.

Total page weight, layout shift and blocking time are budgets a build has to pass before it
ships, so a gallery cannot quietly get heavier release by release. Load time itself we write
against a two-second target and then watch in real visitor data, which is the only place it can
be judged honestly. Your space and your work should look as good on a small screen as they do
in person, and still load before someone gives up. That is an engineering problem with a known
solution, not a trade-off you have to accept.
